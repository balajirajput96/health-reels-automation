# Daily Health-Content Editorial Standard

**Purpose.** This standard governs every future 60-second educational video about psychology, neuroscience, diet, and mental health. The channel may explain research and public-health concepts, but it must not diagnose viewers, prescribe treatment, promise outcomes, or turn preliminary findings into certainty.

## Editorial scope

Each video must address **one narrow, general-audience learning question**. Appropriate examples include how a study design differs from a clinical recommendation, what a commonly used neuroscience term means, or what an evidence-based mental-health resource says about seeking help. Inappropriate topics include individualized symptom interpretation, medication changes, supplement dosages, eating-disorder content without specialist review, claims of cures, or claims that a single intervention will work for every person.

| Required control | Operating rule |
|---|---|
| Evidence hierarchy | Prefer systematic reviews, clinical guidelines, public-health agencies, and primary research from established journals. Treat preprints, influencer commentary, single small studies, and anecdotal reports as insufficient on their own. |
| Claim language | Use calibrated wording such as “research suggests,” “is associated with,” or “in this study.” Never use “proves,” “cures,” “guarantees,” or “works for everyone.” |
| Source traceability | Every factual claim must be mapped to a source URL, publication date, author or institution, and a short claim note before publication. |
| Expert videos | University, professor, clinician, or expert YouTube videos may illustrate ideas, but their claims must be cross-checked against a primary or institutional source. |
| Medical boundary | Do not diagnose, triage, recommend medication changes, give diet plans, calculate dosing, or replace professional care. |
| Sensitive content | Avoid graphic descriptions of self-harm, disordered eating, or trauma. If a topic concerns acute risk, use carefully worded help-seeking language and direct viewers to local emergency or crisis services. |
| Transparency | Add a concise on-screen and caption-level statement that the content is general education, not personal medical advice. |

## Mandatory research and review gate

Before a script can enter production, the workflow must complete the following verification record.

| Gate | Pass condition | Failure action |
|---|---|---|
| Topic eligibility | Topic is general education and does not request a diagnosis, personalized treatment, or dosage | Reject or reframe the topic |
| Source quality | At least two independent high-quality sources support the central claim | Hold for more research |
| YouTube cross-check | Any claim drawn from an expert video has a matching institutional or research source | Remove the claim or cite it as opinion |
| Claim calibration | Script distinguishes association, mechanism hypothesis, and clinical recommendation | Rewrite for precision |
| Harm check | No stigma, sensationalism, coercive language, or unsupported fear appeal | Rewrite or reject |
| Production check | Visuals and narration do not imply a diagnosis, outcome guarantee, or professional relationship | Revise visual and caption language |

## 60-second educational structure

Every script should use this constrained structure: a 3–5 second question-based hook; a 10–15 second definition or context section; a 25–30 second evidence explanation with explicit limits; a 5–10 second practical, non-prescriptive takeaway; and a 3–5 second educational disclaimer. The delivery must be calm, plain-language, and non-sensational.

> **Required disclaimer:** “General education only; it is not medical advice. For personal health concerns, speak with a qualified professional.”

## Source policy

The channel’s reusable source library should prioritize official public-health and research institutions. The U.S. Department of Health and Human Services defines health misinformation as information that is false, inaccurate, or misleading according to the best available evidence, and recommends evidence-based communication practices.[1] The World Health Organization describes how misleading information can harm health choices and emphasizes risk- and evidence-based approaches to communication.[2] The National Institute of Mental Health offers public mental-health information and points audiences to professional help resources rather than individualized social-media guidance.[3]

## Publication metadata standard

Each caption must include a source note or link destination when platform features allow it, a concise educational disclaimer, and no disease, treatment, or outcome promises. Hashtags must be descriptive rather than diagnostic or fear-based. Covers must state only the verified educational topic and must not imply medical authority.
