# Reel 0004 — Cognitive Biases: What Studies Measure

## Research question

When a psychology experiment reports a “cognitive bias,” what is actually being measured? This reel uses a narrow measurement angle: a task score is evidence about performance under a particular task and context; it is not automatically a complete, permanent measure of a person’s character or a universal “bias level.”

## Source 1 — peer-reviewed review

**Berthet, V. (2021).** *The Measurement of Individual Differences in Cognitive Biases: A Review and Improvement.* Frontiers in Psychology, 12, 630177. DOI: [10.3389/fpsyg.2021.630177](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.630177). Accessible full text: [Frontiers article](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.630177/full).

The review examines objective measures based on performance in experimental tasks. Its abstract states that reliable measures exist for some biases, but other key biases still need better measures; the author concludes that measurement of individual differences in cognitive biases remains at an early stage and that contextualized measures need improvement. The review also explains that many results come from group-level experimental comparisons, which should not be silently converted into the claim that every individual shows the same bias to the same degree. It discusses challenges including adapting between-subject designs to within-subject measurement and building reliable composite scores.

**What this supports:** A cognitive-bias task can operationalize a specific response pattern, and measurement reliability/context matter. It does not support labeling every person with a fixed global bias score.

## Source 2 — empirical psychometric study

**Machulska, A., Kleinke, K., & Klucken, T. (2023; published online 2022).** *Same same, but different: A psychometric examination of three frequently used experimental tasks for cognitive bias assessment in a sample of healthy young adults.* Behavior Research Methods, 55, 1332–1351. DOI: [10.3758/s13428-022-01804-9](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-022-01804-9). Accessible article: [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13428-022-01804-9).

The study repeatedly measured approach, attentional, and association biases at five time points in 79 healthy young adults using the Approach-Avoidance Task, visual dot-probe task, and Implicit Association Test, with PC and touchscreen devices. Reliability varied by task, device, and time point. The abstract reports that the dot-probe task appeared completely unreliable in this sample; the PC-based Approach-Avoidance Task and IAT versions showed moderate reliability; almost no associations appeared across tasks or between implicit and explicit measures. The authors recommend routinely reporting psychometric properties and choosing tasks that fit the precise research question.

**What this supports:** Different tasks that are all described as “bias measures” may not behave as interchangeable measures. A score can depend on the task, device, timing, and psychometric quality.

## Source 3 — peer-reviewed review of predictive validity

**Meissner, F., Grigutsch, L. A., Koranyi, N., Müller, F., & Rothermund, K. (2019).** *Predicting Behavior With Implicit Measures: Disillusioning Findings, Reasonable Explanations, and Sophisticated Solutions.* Frontiers in Psychology, 10, 2483. DOI: [10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02483](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02483). PubMed/PMC: [PMID 31787912 / PMC6856205](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6856205/).

This review discusses implicit measures, including the Implicit Association Test, and their relationship to behavior. It reports that meta-analytic findings reviewed in the paper showed low average implicit-criterion correlations and negligible incremental predictive validity beyond self-report in the cited literature. The authors explain that task scores can be influenced by factors such as recoding, the difference between evaluation and motivation, the difference between associations and propositional beliefs, and mismatch between a general predictor and context-specific behavior.

**What this supports:** Even when a task captures a meaningful response pattern, prediction of real-world behavior is a separate validity question. A task result should not be presented as destiny or as a guaranteed forecast of what someone will do.

## Evidence classification and boundaries

The central claim is **peer-reviewed measurement evidence**, not a diagnosis or a personal assessment. The reel should distinguish: (1) an experimental task result, (2) reliability and validity of the measurement, and (3) prediction of behavior. These are different questions. No personal advice, treatment claim, diagnosis, guarantee, or personalized inference is included. The reel should not say that cognitive biases are imaginary; the precise claim is that measurement is task- and context-dependent and that one score is not a complete description of a person.
