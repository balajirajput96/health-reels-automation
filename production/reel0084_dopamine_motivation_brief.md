# REEL-0084 Production Brief

## Concept

A Hindi general-education reel on "Dopamine and Motivation: Why Anticipation Feels Better Than Achievement"—how dopaminergic neurons in the Ventral Tegmental Area (VTA) encode the prediction and anticipation of reward (not the reward itself), creating the constant wanting/seeking drive that underlies motivation, curiosity, addiction, and compulsive scrolling. It attributes findings to Wolfram Schultz, Kent Berridge, and motivational neuroscientists.

## Audience and takeaway

The audience is adults curious about why social media feels irresistible, why winning often feels disappointing, and how reward expectation drives behavior more than reward delivery. The takeaway is that designing your environment to create frequent, earned, small-win dopamine spikes (progress milestones, learning streaks, achievement feedback) is more motivating than chasing rare large rewards.

## Format

Portrait 9:16 video, approximately one minute, with Hindi off-screen narration, synchronized Hindi captions, and topic-specific reference visuals.

## Seven-span visual plan

1. Hook: why buying a new gadget or achieving a big goal feels amazing for a day, and then you immediately start craving the next thing.
2. Dopamine and Prediction Error: Wolfram Schultz's discovery that dopamine neurons fire to reward-predicting cues, not to the reward itself.
3. Wanting vs Liking: Kent Berridge's distinction—dopamine drives wanting/craving, while opioid systems drive actual liking/pleasure.
4. The Anticipation Peak: dopamine rises during the approach to reward, peaks at uncertain anticipation, and drops upon delivery.
5. Social Media and Variable Ratio Reinforcement: slot-machine-style unpredictable reward making dopamine anticipation chronically activated.
6. Engineering Healthy Dopamine: using progress milestones, learning streaks, micro-goals, and meaningful challenge to get sustained motivation spikes.
7. Safety boundary: standard educational disclaimer.

## Evidence and safety gates

Attribute dopamine neuroscience to peer-reviewed literature (Schultz, Berridge, Salamone, Carlezon). Use "dopamine," "VTA," "reward prediction error," "wanting vs liking," and "variable ratio reinforcement." Do not prescribe dopaminergic medications or diagnose addiction.
