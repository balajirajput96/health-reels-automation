# REEL-0163 Production Brief

## Concept

A Hindi general-education reel on "The Spotlight Effect: Egocentric Anchoring, Social Anxiety Bias, and Why Nobody Notices Your Mistakes as Much as You Think"—how humans overestimate the extent to which other people notice their appearance, awkward moments, wardrobe malfunctions, or speech blunders by more than 50% due to egocentric anchoring. It attributes findings to Dr. Thomas Gilovich and Dr. Kenneth Savitsky (Cornell University) and the famous Barry Manilow t-shirt study.

## Audience and takeaway

The audience is socially anxious individuals, public speakers, students, and professionals fearing embarrassment. The takeaway is that everyone is inside their own spotlight thinking about themselves; you are largely free from hyper-scrutiny, so relax and be yourself.

## Format

Portrait 9:16 video, approximately one minute, with Hindi off-screen narration, synchronized Hindi captions, and topic-specific reference visuals.

## Seven-span visual plan

1. Hook: why a small stain on your shirt or a tiny stumble while talking feels like the whole world is staring directly at you.
2. Cornell University's Barry Manilow Experiment: students forced to wear an embarrassing t-shirt into a crowded classroom.
3. The 50% Overestimation Gap: students believed 50% of the room noticed the shirt, but only 23% actually saw it.
4. Egocentric Anchoring Mechanism: we are the central protagonist of our own conscious experience, so our brain falsely assumes everyone else is watching our movie too.
5. The Universal Spotlight Truth: other people are too busy worrying about their own stains, awkwardness, and self-image to judge yours.
6. The Anxiety Liberator Rule: next time you feel embarrassed, remember: nobody is watching as closely as you think. Take a breath and let it go.
7. Safety boundary: standard educational disclosure.

## Evidence and safety gates

Attribute social psychology to peer-reviewed literature (Gilovich, Savitsky, Medvec, Epley). Use "The Spotlight Effect," "Egocentric Anchoring," "Social Anxiety Bias," and "Cornell Study."
