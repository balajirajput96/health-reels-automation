**Topic question:** How can morning daylight act as a timing cue for the body clock?

## Editorial frame

This Reel explains that light is an environmental cue for circadian timing. It does **not** diagnose sleep problems, promise improved sleep, prescribe exposure duration, or instruct viewers to use light as treatment. The message remains conditional and general: morning daylight is one timing cue among several, and sleep is affected by more than light alone.[1] [2] [3]

## Evidence mapping and review decision

| Narrated proposition | Source support | Script safeguard |
|---|---|---|
| Circadian rhythm organizes approximately 24-hour patterns. | CDC/NIOSH light-and-circadian training; peer-reviewed review.[1] [2] | Avoids calling it a diagnosis or personal health metric. |
| Light and dark are timing cues communicated through retinal pathways to the brain’s central clock. | Blume et al. review; Yale Medicine explainer.[2] [4] | Says “master clock” only as a high-level educational metaphor, not a treatment mechanism. |
| Morning bright light can shift timing earlier for people on regular night-sleep schedules. | CDC/NIOSH; Wright et al.; Blume et al.[1] [2] [3] | Uses “can” and “associate,” and retains the schedule qualifier. |
| Evening bright light often has the opposite directional effect. | CDC/NIOSH; Blume et al.[1] [2] | Does not attribute sleep problems to a single behavior. |
| Light is one cue and sleep has multiple influences. | Blume et al. notes confounding and multiple sleep-regulation factors.[2] | Prevents an overpromising takeaway. |
