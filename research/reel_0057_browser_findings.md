# Reel 0057 browser/research findings

## Scope
Topic: working memory — what studies actually measure. Planned angle: span and n-back tasks are task-specific indicators and cannot be treated as interchangeable or as a complete readout of a person’s memory capacity.

## Source 1 — Redick & Lindsey (2013)
DOI: 10.3758/s13423-013-0453-9
URL: https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-013-0453-9
Title: Complex span and n-back measures of working memory: A meta-analysis. *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*, 20, 1109–1133.

The publisher page’s accessible abstract states that complex span and n-back tasks were weakly correlated, with significant heterogeneity across studies. It concludes that the two task families cannot be used interchangeably as working-memory measures in research applications. The article also reports that stimulus domain, such as verbal versus visuospatial content, influenced the magnitude of the relationship. This directly supports a task- and format-bounded interpretation.

## Evidence and safety boundary

Established evidence: working-memory research uses task performance such as span, accuracy, response time, updating, and recognition; different task families can correlate imperfectly and may be affected by stimulus domain and procedure. Theory/model: these task results are used to study working-memory processes, but a single score is not a complete measure of a person’s general memory, intelligence, or everyday functioning. No diagnosis, treatment, individual prediction, or guaranteed cognitive assessment is made.

## Additional verified sources

**Conway, Kane, Bunting, Hambrick, Wilhelm, & Engle (2005)**, “Working memory span tasks: A methodological review and user’s guide,” *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*, 12, 769–786. DOI: [10.3758/BF03196772](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03196772). The publisher abstract says counting, operation, and reading span tasks are widely used measures of working-memory capacity, and the review examines their reliability, validity, administration, scoring, and statistical treatment. Safe use: span scores operationalize performance on specified procedures; they are not a complete measure of general cognition or everyday memory.

**Schmiedek, Lövdén, & Lindenberger (2014)**, “A task is a task is a task: putting complex span, n-back, and other working memory indicators in psychometric context,” *Frontiers in Psychology*, 5, 1475. DOI: [10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01475](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01475). The accessible article emphasizes measurement error, task-specific variance, and paradigm-specific variance. It reports a hierarchical latent-factor analysis and stresses going beyond single tasks and paradigms when studying higher-order constructs. This supports the reel’s distinction between a test score, task-specific variance, and a broader theoretical construct.

## Self-report strategy source

**Ritakallio, Fellman, Salmi, Jylkkä, & Laine (2024)**, “Self-reported strategy use in working memory tasks,” *Scientific Reports*, 14, 4893. DOI: [10.1038/s41598-024-54160-3](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-54160-3). The open-access article reports that participants’ open-ended strategy reports were coded into a three-level strategy-sophistication measure: no strategy, maintenance strategy, and manipulation strategy. It describes strategy sophistication as a reported/coded measure and separately analyzes objective memory performance. This supports keeping self-report strategy distinct from task accuracy and from a global claim about a person’s working memory.
