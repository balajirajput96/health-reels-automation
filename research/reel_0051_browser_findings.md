# Reel 0051 retrieval audit: feedback and what studies measure

## Retrieval scope

The retrieval focused on peer-reviewed research that separates immediate performance from later retention or transfer, and feedback timing from feedback content and learner interpretation.

## Verified findings

**Mullet, Butler, Verdin, von Borries, and Marsh (2014), Journal of Applied Research in Memory and Cognition, DOI 10.1016/j.jarmac.2014.05.001.** In a pair of experiments embedded in an upper-level college engineering course, students practiced applying complex concepts in weekly homework. Feedback was delivered immediately after the deadline or one week later. Delayed feedback was followed by better performance on subsequent course exams containing new problems about the same concepts, indicating superior transfer in that setting. Students nevertheless reported that immediate feedback helped them more, showing a mismatch between perceived and measured effectiveness. This is a course-specific result, not a universal rule that delayed feedback is always superior.

**Taxipulati and Lu (2021), Frontiers in Psychology, DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2021.706821.** This experiment randomly assigned 157 college students to six combinations of feedback timing and content in a multimedia learning task: immediate or delayed feedback, paired with adaptive, elaborated, or knowledge-of-correct-response feedback. The study measured test-stage problem-solving, motivation, cognitive-load reports, and eye-movement indicators. Immediate feedback produced higher test scores than delayed feedback in this task, and adaptive feedback outperformed the other two content conditions. The result demonstrates that timing and content can interact with task design; it does not contradict the engineering-course finding as if one timing were universally best.

**Van der Kleij, Feskens, and Eggen (2015), Review of Educational Research, DOI 10.3102/0034654314564881.** A meta-analysis of 40 studies and 70 effect sizes examined item-based feedback in computer-based learning. Elaborated feedback had a larger average effect size than correctness-only or correct-answer feedback, especially for higher-order outcomes. The abstract reports effect sizes of 0.49 for elaborated feedback, 0.05 for knowledge of results, and 0.32 for knowledge of the correct response. Timing and educational level also moderated outcomes, while the evidence did not establish a simple universal immediate-versus-delayed rule.

**Fyfe, Borriello, and Merrick (2022), Educational Psychologist, DOI 10.1080/00461520.2022.2108426.** A systematic review of 44 peer-reviewed papers and 59 unique experiments examined task-level corrective feedback for children in literacy, mathematics, and problem solving. The review separates feedback from the learner’s noticing, interpreting, integrating, and acting on the message, and emphasizes that feedback effects depend on age, prior knowledge, message features, and learner differences. It is useful for defining feedback processing, but its population is children and should not be generalized automatically to adults.

## Evidence boundaries

Feedback is not a single intervention. Timing, content, task complexity, prior knowledge, learner age, whether feedback is noticed, and the final outcome measure can all matter. Immediate test performance, delayed retention, transfer to new problems, motivation, cognitive-load reports, eye movements, and student preferences are different observables. A result favoring immediate feedback in one multimedia task and a result favoring delayed feedback in one engineering course cannot be collapsed into a universal prescription.
