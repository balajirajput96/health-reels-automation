# Reel 0026 — altered states: what do studies actually measure?

## Evidence question

This reel asks how research operationalizes an altered state of consciousness (ASC). It does not ask whether a state is spiritually true, whether a person is healthy or ill, or whether a scan can read private experience. The evidence-bounded angle is that ASC research usually combines first-person phenomenology with behavioral, physiological, neuroimaging, or computational measures, and each layer answers a different question.

## Vetted evidence map

| Evidence point | What the source supports | Boundary for narration |
|---|---|---|
| ASC is multidimensional and can involve changes in perception, emotion, thoughts, self-experience, time, or space | Reviews and databases describe ASC as qualitative, multidimensional changes rather than a single scalar level | Do not claim that one universal ASC score exists |
| Standardized retrospective questionnaires quantify reported phenomenology | The Altered States Database collects questionnaire data from peer-reviewed human studies and includes instruments such as APZ/5D-ASC/11D-ASC, PCI, HRS, and MEQ30 | A questionnaire measures reported experience in a defined instrument, not the complete state or its cause |
| Psychometric structure is testable and may be refined | Studerus, Gamma, and Vollenweider evaluated OAV data from psilocybin-, ketamine-, and MDMA-induced experiences; the original factor model did not fit well, and lower-order factors were constructed with reported reliability and validity evidence | Do not present the factor labels as timeless, universal biological modules |
| Neural measures are correlates or signatures under a study design | fMRI, EEG/MEG, connectivity, blood-flow, and complexity measures can be compared with subjective reports or behavioral tasks | Correlation or model fit is not direct mind-reading, and it does not by itself establish a subjective cause |
| Systematic evidence has scope limits | The 2022 ASDB update used PRISMA-guided searching and included 165 journal articles with extracted psychometric data; its dataset is a research resource, not a complete theory of consciousness | State that instruments, induction method, timing, sample, and analysis constrain interpretation |

## Primary and review sources

1. Studerus, E., Gamma, A., & Vollenweider, F. X. (2010). “Psychometric Evaluation of the Altered States of Consciousness Rating Scale (OAV).” *PLOS ONE*, 5(8), e12412. DOI: [10.1371/journal.pone.0012412](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0012412). The article reports that the original OAV model did not fit well in pooled experimentally induced ASC data and evaluates lower-order scales; it also explains that the instrument is retrospective self-report of subjective experience.

2. Schmidt, T. T., & Berkemeyer, H. (2018). “The Altered States Database: Psychometric Data of Altered States of Consciousness.” *Frontiers in Psychology*, 9, 1028. DOI: [10.3389/fpsyg.2018.01028](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.01028). The article describes an open database of questionnaire data from peer-reviewed human ASC studies and emphasizes multidimensional phenomenological patterns and comparison across induction methods.

3. Prugger, J., Derdiyok, E., Dinkelacker, J., Costines, C., et al. (2022). “The Altered States Database: Psychometric data from a systematic literature review.” *Scientific Data*, 9, 720. DOI: [10.1038/s41597-022-01822-4](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41597-022-01822-4). The PRISMA-guided systematic review describes the included questionnaires, extraction process, 165 included journal articles, and limitations of the evidence base.

4. Carhart-Harris, R. L., Erritzoe, D., Williams, T., Stone, J. M., Reed, L. J., Colasanti, A., et al. (2012). “Neural correlates of the psychedelic state as determined by fMRI studies with psilocybin.” *PNAS*, 109(6), 2138–2143. DOI: [10.1073/pnas.1119598109](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1119598109). The study used arterial-spin-labeling and BOLD fMRI in healthy volunteers and reported that measured changes, including in connector regions, related to the intensity of subjective effects. The reel will describe this as a study-specific neural correlate, not proof that fMRI reads a person’s experience directly.

5. Cofré, R., Herzog, R., Mediano, P. A. M., Piccinini, J., Rosas, F. E., Sanz Perl, Y., & Tagliazucchi, E. (2020). “Whole-Brain Models to Explore Altered States of Consciousness from the Bottom Up.” *Brain Sciences*, 10(9), 626. DOI: [10.3390/brainsci10090626](https://doi.org/10.3390/brainsci10090626). This review discusses subjective versus access consciousness, the multidimensional and sometimes ambiguous definition of ASC, and candidate signatures from EEG, MEG, fMRI, connectivity, and complexity. It explicitly frames the field as an active research program rather than a settled single measure.

## Research and safety limits

The selected papers do not justify diagnosing a viewer, predicting an individual’s mental state, prescribing a substance or practice, or claiming that meditation, psychedelics, dreams, hypnosis, or unusual experiences have one guaranteed meaning. The reel will avoid dose advice, treatment claims, clinical labels, spiritual certainty, and literal mind-reading. It will distinguish retrospective self-report, observable behavior, physiological or imaging signals, and model-derived summaries. AI narration and deterministic conceptual visuals will be disclosed.
