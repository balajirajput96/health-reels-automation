# Reel 0066 browser-verified findings — Deep-work claims: what studies measure

## Source 1: Langner and Eickhoff (2012), “Sustaining Attention to Simple Tasks: A Meta-Analytic Review of the Neural Mechanisms of Vigilant Attention,” *Psychological Bulletin*, DOI 10.1037/a0030694

URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3627747/

The review defines vigilant attention as sustaining efficient conscious stimulus processing over more than a short period, especially in simple or repetitive tasks. It summarizes evidence that performance can decline as time-on-task increases, and that simple repetitive tasks can be especially demanding. It treats sustained attention as multi-component rather than a single unitary faculty, with sustained or recurrent processes supporting task-set and arousal maintenance and transient processes supporting target-driven reorienting.

The safe measurement interpretation is that vigilance or continuous-performance paradigms observe behavior over time, often through detection or discrimination accuracy, reaction time, omissions, and time-on-task trends. Neuroimaging findings identify task-associated network activity, but those signals are correlates of the task and design, not a direct measurement of “deep work” or a private mental state.

## Source 2: Hasan (2024), “Digital multitasking and hyperactivity: unveiling the hidden costs to brain health,” *Annals of Medicine and Surgery*, DOI 10.1097/MS9.0000000000002576

URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11543232/

This open-access article discusses digital multitasking as simultaneous administration of multiple digital tasks and reviews possible cognitive costs such as increased cognitive load, divided resources, reduced concentration, and poorer task performance. It cites task-switching and media-multitasking work, but its broad framing is not itself a controlled proof that every digital interruption produces the same loss for every person or task.

The safe use in this reel is limited to the distinction between a controlled multitasking or interruption manipulation and broad “productivity” language. Claims must specify whether the evidence concerns concurrent multitasking, sequential switching, a particular device or task, or an editorial synthesis. Avoid repeating broad numerical claims unless verified directly in the original study and matched to the exact design.

## Safe operational categories

**Sustained-attention performance:** accuracy, reaction time, omissions, false alarms, variability, and change across time-on-task in a specified vigilance or continuous-performance task.

**Interruption or switching cost:** a difference between uninterrupted and interrupted conditions, or between task-switch and repeat conditions, under controlled timing and instructions.

**Self-report or subjective effort:** a participant’s rating of effort, concentration, fatigue, or perceived productivity. It is not identical to objective task performance.

**Neural or physiological correlates:** fMRI, EEG, eye movements, or other signals associated with task engagement or reported state. They are indirect, context-bound correlates.

**Generalization limit:** “deep work” is a popular umbrella label, not a single standardized laboratory variable. A result from a simple repetitive vigilance task or a particular digital multitasking setup does not automatically establish a universal productivity law.

## Boundary conclusion

Studies relevant to deep-work claims usually measure a defined task, time-on-task pattern, interruption or switching manipulation, response accuracy and speed, self-report, or task-associated neural activity. These observables can inform sustained attention, interference, workload, or performance under the tested conditions. They do not directly measure a hidden state called “deep work,” prove that uninterrupted work is always superior for every task, or justify judging a person’s character or clinical status from one score.

## Source 3: Westbrook et al. (2018), “Task errors by emergency physicians are associated with interruptions, multitasking, fatigue and working memory capacity: a prospective, direct observation study,” *BMJ Quality & Safety*, DOI 10.1136/bmjqs-2017-007333

URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6204927/

This prospective direct-observation study followed emergency physicians in a clinical setting and measured prescribing errors alongside observed interruptions, multitasking, sleep, and a working-memory-capacity measure. The reported outcome was prescribing-error rate in that specific clinical context. Interruptions, multitasking, and poor sleep were associated with increased error rates, while working-memory capacity was described as mitigating the influence to an extent.

This is a context-specific association, not a randomized universal test of “deep work.” It cannot establish that every interruption harms every task, that uninterrupted work is always optimal, or that a person’s performance in this clinical setting defines their general productivity or character. The study is useful for showing why real-world ecological evidence should name the population, activity, outcome, and design rather than collapse everything into a broad productivity slogan.
