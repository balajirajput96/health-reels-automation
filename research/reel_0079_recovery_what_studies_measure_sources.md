## Evidence synthesis

The reviewed literature supports a measurement-focused explanation rather than a universal “recovery score.” A study may operationalize recovery as a change from a stress period to a post-stress period, an affect trajectory, a self-reported ability to recover, a physiological signal measured over a defined window, or functioning after an experience. These are related but not interchangeable constructs.

Repeated experience-sampling or daily-diary designs can collect feelings, behavior, context, and events at several times. A static trait questionnaire can capture a person's reported trait-like view of resilience, but it cannot by itself capture a dynamic recovery trajectory. Laboratory and daily-life measures also depend on the stressor, timing, context, and outcome, so a laboratory score should not automatically be treated as a daily-life score.

Heart rate, vagally mediated heart-rate variability, skin conductance, cortisol, and body temperature are examples of physiological measures used in stress research. They are correlates collected under specified conditions, not direct readouts of thoughts, character, mental health, or a diagnosis. Self-report measures describe the participant's reported experience and do not replace physiology or behavior. Associations with sleep, optimism, or mental-health outcomes do not by themselves establish causation.

## Source 1

**Authors:** Melanie Bamert and Jennifer Inauen.
**Title:** *Physiological stress reactivity and recovery: Some laboratory results transfer to daily life.*
**Journal:** Frontiers in Psychology.
**DOI:** [10.3389/fpsyg.2022.943065](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.943065)
**Opened full text:** [PMC9421134](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9421134/)

**Used claim:** Laboratory research can examine physiological reactivity and recovery with controlled timing, while daily-life recovery is affected by context, behavior, and the pile-up of stressors. Aggregated time windows can miss event-level dynamics.

**Boundary:** vmHRV is a physiological correlate influenced by many factors; it is not a unique psychological-state detector.

## Source 2

**Authors:** Whitney Tramel, Ben Schram, Elisa Canetti, and Robin Orr.
**Title:** *An Examination of Subjective and Objective Measures of Stress in Tactical Populations: A Scoping Review.*
**Journal:** Healthcare, 11(18), 2515.
**DOI:** [10.3390/healthcare11182515](https://doi.org/10.3390/healthcare11182515)
**Opened full text:** [MDPI article](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-9032/11/18/2515)

**Used claim:** The review describes objective outcomes such as heart rate, cortisol, and body temperature, and subjective measures such as perceived exertion and anxiety inventories; studies often combine types of measures.

**Boundary:** This is a review of tactical populations and stress outcomes. It does not show that a single marker universally measures recovery for every person.

## Source 3

**Authors:** Juul Zietse, Loes Keijsers, Manon H.J. Hillegers, Annabel Vreeker, Anne-Laura van Harmelen, and Lianne P. de Vries.
**Title:** *Daily resilience: A systematic review of measures and associations with well-being and mental health in experience sampling studies.*
**Journal:** Development and Psychopathology, 38(1), 130–155.
**DOI:** [10.1017/S0954579425000197](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0954579425000197)
**Opened full text:** [Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/development-and-psychopathology/article/daily-resilience-a-systematic-review-of-measures-and-associations-with-wellbeing-and-mental-health-in-experience-sampling-studies/DF3BF7ABADBCC46C175FF544F3BB1B4C)

**Used claim:** The systematic review distinguishes self-reported ability to recover from affective recovery after daily stressors. It explains why repeated real-time measurements are needed for dynamic recovery, while static trait measures cannot capture the process alone.

**Boundary:** Reported associations with sleep, optimism, or mental-health outcomes are not causal proof and do not justify labeling an individual from one observation.

## Source 4

**Authors:** Joana De Calheiros Velozo, Thomas Vaessen, Ginette Lafit, Stephan Claes, and Inez Myin-Germeys.
**Title:** *Is daily-life stress reactivity a measure of stress recovery? An investigation of laboratory and daily-life stress.*
**Journal:** Stress and Health, 39(3), 638–650.
**DOI:** [10.1002/smi.3213](https://doi.org/10.1002/smi.3213)
**Opened record:** [University of Twente Research Information](https://research.utwente.nl/en/publications/is-daily-life-stress-reactivity-a-measure-of-stress-recovery-anin/)

**Used claim:** Laboratory reactivity and recovery were computed from specified comparisons, and the study compared them with event-related daily-life stress measures using negative affect, heart rate, HRV, and skin conductance across a laboratory task and eight days of experience sampling. The abstract reports little strong correspondence across contexts except for a specific affective result.

**Boundary:** A laboratory recovery score and a daily-life response are not automatically equivalent; the context and sampling design matter.

## Measurement map for production

| Construct | Example measurement | Safe interpretation | Not established alone |
|---|---|---|---|
| Subjective experience | Self-report of affect, stress, perceived exertion, or anxiety | What the participant reports feeling at that time | A complete physiological state or diagnosis |
| Affective recovery | Change or trajectory in positive or negative affect after a stressor | A defined emotional pattern over a defined interval | A permanent trait or guaranteed outcome |
| Physiology | Heart rate, HRV, skin conductance, cortisol, or temperature | A signal recorded under specified conditions | Direct mind-reading, character, or causal mechanism |
| Functioning outcome | Task performance or later well-being indicator | Performance or outcome in that study's design | Proof of why recovery differed |
| Time and context | Baseline/control, stress period, post-stress window, ESM prompts | The comparison and trajectory being analyzed | A universal one-number score |

## Allowed claims in the reel

The reel may say that recovery is defined operationally, that time and context matter, that studies can combine self-report with physiological signals, that repeated daily-life sampling can trace change, and that lab and everyday measures need not match.

## Excluded claims

The reel must not diagnose stress, anxiety, depression, trauma, or resilience; recommend treatment, supplements, meditation, breathing, or other personal interventions; promise faster recovery; claim that HRV, cortisol, or any single biomarker reads the mind; imply that recovery speed is a moral quality; or present correlations as causal effects.
