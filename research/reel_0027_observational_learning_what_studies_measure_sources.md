# Reel 0027 — observational learning: studies actually measure what?

## Evidence question

This reel explains how observational learning is operationalized in experiments. It distinguishes merely seeing an action, copying an action, learning an action–outcome or stimulus–response association, later transfer to a new choice, computational parameters, and task-related neural correlates. It does not claim that observing someone reveals their intentions, personality, intelligence, or private thoughts.

## Vetted evidence map

| Evidence point | What the source supports | Boundary for narration |
|---|---|---|
| Observational learning is more than simple imitation | Reviews describe learning by watching another person’s actions and associated outcomes, then using that information to guide later behavior | Do not equate copying with complete learning |
| Behavioral tasks measure later performance | Experiments can compare choice accuracy, response selection, rule retrieval, motor reproduction, delayed transfer, or performance across trials | Performance on one task is not a global learning ability |
| Outcomes and consequences matter | Classic behavioral work and modern tasks compare what the observed model did and what happened afterward | Do not claim observation always produces the same result or that copying is automatic |
| Computational models summarize trial-by-trial updating | Reinforcement-learning models can estimate prediction errors, learning rates, and choice specificity from repeated choices | Model parameters are estimates under assumptions, not directly observed mental quantities |
| Neural measures are correlates within a design | fMRI and reviews relate task variables such as prediction errors, observed outcomes, and rule retrieval to brain activity | Neural activation does not read a person’s thoughts and does not prove causation alone |

## Primary and review sources

1. Kang, W., Pineda Hernández, S., & Mei, J. (2021). “Neural Mechanisms of Observational Learning: A Neural Working Model.” *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience*, 14, 609312. DOI: [10.3389/fnhum.2020.609312](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2020.609312). The review defines observational learning as using observed actions and outcomes to build stimulus–response–outcome associations, distinguishes it from simple imitation, and surveys behavioral, computational, and neuroimaging task designs.

2. Fryling, M. J., Johnston, C., & Hayes, L. J. (2011). “Understanding Observational Learning: An Interbehavioral Approach.” *The Analysis of Verbal Behavior*, 27(1), 191–203. DOI: [10.1007/BF03393102](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF03393102). This peer-reviewed review discusses classic modeling research, behavior change after observation, later demonstrations, and the role of consequences. It also emphasizes that theories and operational definitions differ.

3. Rodriguez Buritica, J. M., Eppinger, B., Heekeren, H. R., Crone, E. A., & van Duijvenvoorde, A. C. K. (2024). “Observational reinforcement learning in children and young adults.” *npj Science of Learning*, 9, 18. DOI: [10.1038/s41539-024-00227-9](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41539-024-00227-9). This primary study used repeated probabilistic choices, observational versus individual learning conditions, reinforcement-learning modeling, and model-based fMRI in children and young adults. The reported findings associate observed-outcome prediction errors with task-specific neural activity and learning performance; they do not establish a universal neural signature.

4. Olsson, A., Knapska, E., & Lindström, B. (2020). “The neural and computational systems of social learning.” *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*, 21, 197–212. DOI: [10.1038/s41583-020-0276-4](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41583-020-0276-4). This review is cited by the recent primary study as a broader account of social-learning neural and computational systems. The reel uses it only for the general distinction between social learning mechanisms and individual learning, not for a simplified brain-region claim.

## Research and safety limits

Observational-learning findings depend on task design, stimulus and outcome structure, participant age and experience, timing, sample, and model assumptions. A viewer’s ability on one copying, choice, or memory task cannot diagnose a learning disorder or define intelligence or personality. The reel will avoid personal assessment, clinical labels, manipulation instructions, or claims that observing another person gives access to their mind. AI narration and deterministic conceptual visuals will be disclosed.
