# Reel 0056 browser/research findings

## Scope
Topic: deliberation — what studies actually measure. Planned angle: the label “deliberation” is broader than a single response-time or self-report measure; conclusions should remain bounded by the observable and model.

## Source 1 — De Neys (2025)
DOI: 10.1038/s44159-025-00466-6
URL: https://www.nature.com/articles/s44159-025-00466-6
Title: Defining deliberation for dual-process models of reasoning. *Nature Reviews Psychology*.

The perspective says deliberation is often called “system 2” thinking but its precise conceptualization has received limited attention. It presents four complementary functions: response control, response generation, response justification, and regulation. This supports a measure boundary: a longer response time, a self-report of thinking, or an accurate answer may be relevant evidence in a task, but none alone establishes every function of deliberation or reveals a hidden process directly.

## Source 2 — Bhui, Kira, and Loewenstein (2019)
Title: Mechanisms of deliberation during preferential choice. *Cognition*, 190, 14–27. PubMed Central record: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6329476/

Search results identify this peer-reviewed paper as a study of deliberation during preferential choice using behavioral choice patterns and computational modeling. The browser page encountered a reCAPTCHA barrier, so no claim beyond the accessible title/abstract-level description is used in the reel. The planned evidence language will say that computational models can formalize patterns such as choices and decision times under assumptions, not that model parameters are literal scans of thought.

## Source 3 — Fleming & Lau (2014)
DOI: 10.1098/rstb.2013.0206
URL: https://royalsocietypublishing.org/rstb/article/367/1594/1310/21855/Metacognition-in-human-decision-making-confidence
Title: Metacognition in human decision-making: confidence and performance monitoring.

The review concerns confidence and performance monitoring as metacognitive observables in decision tasks. It supports separating confidence reports from objective task performance and from the underlying deliberative process. Confidence can be studied as a judgment about one’s own decision, but is not by itself proof that deliberation occurred.

## Evidence and safety boundary
Established evidence: deliberation has multiple theoretical functions, and studies can observe response time, choice, accuracy, confidence, self-report, and model-derived parameters. Association/theory: mappings from an observable to an underlying process remain theory- and task-dependent. No direct mind-reading, diagnosis, treatment, personal advice, guaranteed prediction, or universal claim that slow or accurate decisions are necessarily deliberative.

## Newly verified details

The confidence review is **Yeung, N., & Summerfield, C. (2012).** Metacognition in human decision-making: confidence and error monitoring. *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B, 367*(1594), 1310–1321. DOI: [10.1098/rstb.2011.0416](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2011.0416). Source ID: `doi:10.1098/rstb.2011.0416`. Its abstract says confidence reports can correlate with objective performance, while also noting that the literature has focused narrowly on simple discrete decisions and may not scale directly to fluid real-world decisions. The article discusses reaction-time distributions and drift-diffusion models as formal accounts of choice latencies, but these are model-based descriptions under assumptions rather than literal recordings of thought.

The De Neys (2025) page confirms the article is a *Nature Reviews Psychology* Perspective, volume 4, pages 544–552, published 19 June 2025. Its abstract explicitly names four deliberation functions: response control, response generation, response justification, and regulation. The paper’s central contribution is conceptual organization, not a single universal behavioral test.

The Bhui, Kira, and Loewenstein paper was not used for exact quantitative claims because the browser encountered a reCAPTCHA barrier at the PMC page. It remains an optional background lead, not a core source ID for the reel.

## Additional verified studies

**Stupple, Pitchford, Ball, Hunt, & Steel (2017)**, *Slower is not always better: Response-time evidence clarifies the limited role of miserly information processing in the Cognitive Reflection Test*, *PLOS ONE*, 12(11), e0186404. DOI: [10.1371/journal.pone.0186404](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0186404). The open-access abstract reports N=391 and says the study examined response times and accuracy on the three-item CRT. It found only a weak correlation between response times and accuracy, with some item-level analyses failing to show the predicted response-time differences. The safe reel claim is therefore that slower response time is an observable task measure, not a universal proof of better or more deliberative thinking.

**Pennycook, Fugelsang, & Koehler (2015)**, *What makes us think? A three-stage dual-process model of analytic engagement*, *Cognitive Psychology*, 80, 34–72. DOI: [10.1016/j.cogpsych.2015.05.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogpsych.2015.05.001). The accessible article preview describes a three-stage model: Type 1 outputs, conflict detection, and Type 2 processing that may rationalize or decouple. Its abstract says four experiments tested a base-rate task and supported dissociable sources of Type 2 processing, while conflict detection can fail. This is presented as a model tested in particular tasks, not a direct instrument for reading an individual’s hidden deliberation.
