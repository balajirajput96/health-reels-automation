# Reel 0034 source-retrieval findings

## Vetted source findings

Troy, Shallcross, Brunner, Friedman, and Jones (2018), *Emotion*, DOI 10.1037/emo0000371, is available through PMC and APA. The study used a within-subjects laboratory design in two samples totaling N=142. Participants used cognitive reappraisal or acceptance during and after sad film clips. Outcomes included subjective negative and positive emotion, physiological responses, and perceived regulatory effort, difficulty, and success. The abstract reports that reappraisal and acceptance showed different short-term patterns: reappraisal was associated with larger changes in reported emotions, while acceptance was perceived as less difficult and showed a different skin-conductance result in one sample. The source therefore supports the claim that “effective” depends on which outcome is measured and that subjective emotion and physiology should not be treated as identical.

Webb, Miles, and Sheeran (2012), *Psychological Bulletin*, DOI 10.1037/a0027600, PubMed PMID 22582737. This is a meta-analysis of 306 experimental comparisons derived from the process model of emotion regulation. It examined experiential, behavioral, and physiological outcomes. The abstract reports that effectiveness differed across processes and within processes, including distinctions among kinds of cognitive change; reappraising the emotional stimulus and perspective taking were not treated as interchangeable with reappraising the emotional response. Moderators included the emotion being regulated, frequency and intended purpose of the strategy, study design, and study characteristics. The reel will not repeat individual effect sizes as universal guarantees; it will use the source to show why operationalization and moderators matter.

Gross and John (2003), *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.85.2.348, PubMed PMID 12916575. Across five studies, the authors introduced measures of habitual reappraisal and suppression and examined validity, affect, relationships, and well-being. The source supports distinguishing a questionnaire about habitual strategy use from an experiment that instructs a strategy in a specific situation. It reports associations, not proof that a questionnaire score causes later outcomes.

## Claim boundaries

Cognitive reappraisal will be described as a strategy or instruction that changes the interpretation/appraisal of an emotional situation. The reel will distinguish strategy use or tendency, momentary self-reported emotion, physiological measures, behavioral/task outcomes, and perceived effort. It will use “associated with,” “in this experiment,” or “in this meta-analysis,” not “always works.” It will not diagnose, prescribe therapy or mindfulness, infer a viewer’s private state, promise symptom relief, or present a single questionnaire score as a complete measure of emotion regulation.
