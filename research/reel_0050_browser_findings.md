# Reel 0050 retrieval audit: desirable difficulty and what studies measure

## Retrieval scope

The retrieval focused on peer-reviewed sources that distinguish effort during learning from learning outcomes measured later, and that separate self-reported effort, immediate performance, delayed retention, and transfer to untrained material.

## Verified findings

**de Bruin and colleagues (2023), Educational Psychology Review, DOI 10.1007/s10648-023-09766-w.** The S2D2 framework defines desirable difficulties as learning conditions that are experienced as effortful yet can improve learning and transfer when difficulty is appropriate. The review emphasizes that learners may infer low learning from high effort and may avoid effective strategies because benefits are delayed or not immediately visible. It also describes retrieval practice, interleaving, spaced learning, varying contexts, and self-explanation as examples, while stressing self-regulation and the need to match conditions to the learning task.

**Maddox and colleagues (2018), Memory & Cognition, DOI 10.3758/s13421-018-0843-3.** Three experiments examined spacing and reminding difficulty using recognition and recall outcomes. The work operationalized difficulty with accuracy and response latency during acquisition, then compared those measures with later memory. Across the experiments, lag effects appeared in final memory even when repetition-detection difficulty was comparable between selected lag conditions. The paper therefore cautions against treating a single proxy of effort or response latency as a complete explanation of later memory.

**Latimier and colleagues (2019), NPJ Science of Learning, DOI 10.1038/s41539-019-0053-1.** In a study of pre-testing, post-testing, and rereading with a seven-day retention test, post-testing produced stronger retention than pre-testing and also promoted transfer to previously untested questions, whereas pre-testing did not show the same transfer pattern. The result supports separating the timing of retrieval practice and measuring transfer rather than only trained-item recall.

## Evidence boundaries

“Harder” is not automatically “better.” A desirable difficulty requires an appropriate task and a later outcome showing learning or transfer; perceived effort alone is not sufficient. Practice accuracy, response time, confidence, and effort ratings are different observables from delayed retention and novel-item transfer. The sources also do not establish one universal study timetable or guarantee a benefit for every learner, material, or context. The reel is educational content only and does not provide individualized instruction, diagnosis, or treatment.
