# Reel 0030 — prosocial behavior: studies actually measure what?

## Evidence question

This reel asks how research measures prosocial behavior without treating kindness as a single hidden trait. It distinguishes self-reported empathy or intention from observed helping, sharing or allocation choices, cooperation in a social task, costly giving, and physiological or neural correlates.

## Vetted evidence map

| Evidence point | What the source supports | Boundary for narration |
|---|---|---|
| Prosocial behavior includes multiple actions | Reviews and methods guides discuss helping, comforting, sharing, cooperation, allocation, and social-dilemma behavior | Do not present one task as the definition of kindness or altruism |
| Empathy and action are distinct | A person’s sensitivity to another’s state can be related to care, but it is not identical to actually helping or comforting | Self-reported empathy is not direct evidence of helping behavior |
| Economic games are controlled behavioral tasks | Dictator, public-goods, trust, or related games record allocations and choices under specified incentives and social structure | A game choice is context-specific and not a complete measure of character |
| Observation and real-world outcomes differ | Lab tasks provide control; field observations and reports capture other aspects but may involve different confounds | Do not generalize a lab allocation to all everyday conduct |
| Biological signals are correlates | Physiological or neural measures can be related to processing and motivation in a task | A signal does not read compassion, intent, or moral worth directly |

## Primary and review sources

1. Decety, J., Bartal, I. B.-A., Uzefovsky, F., & Knafo-Noam, A. (2016). “Empathy as a driver of prosocial behaviour: highly conserved neurobehavioural mechanisms across species.” *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B*, 371(1686), 20150077. DOI: [10.1098/rstb.2015.0077](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2015.0077). This review emphasizes that empathy is used for heterogeneous phenomena and distinguishes sensitivity to others’ states from possible outcomes such as helping or comforting. It discusses behavioral and neurobiological mechanisms while not reducing prosocial action to a single brain signal.

2. Thielmann, I., Böhm, R., Ott, M., & Hilbig, B. E. (2021). “Economic Games: An Introduction and Guide for Research.” *Collabra: Psychology*, 7(1), 19004. DOI: [10.1525/collabra.19004](https://doi.org/10.1525/collabra.19004). This methods guide explains that economic games are widely used to measure prosocial behavior in controlled settings and that game structure creates specific situational affordances. It presents standardized game instructions and stresses theory-driven interpretation.

3. Penner, L. A., Dovidio, J. F., Piliavin, J. A., & Schroeder, D. A. (2005). “Prosocial behavior: Multilevel perspectives.” *Annual Review of Psychology*, 56, 365–392. DOI: [10.1146/annurev.psych.56.091103.070141](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.psych.56.091103.070141). This review frames prosocial behavior across individual, interpersonal, and societal levels, supporting the distinction between immediate helping acts, relationships, and broader systems rather than a single trait label.

4. Eisenberg, N., Fabes, R. A., & Spinrad, T. L. (2006). “Prosocial Development.” In *Handbook of Child Psychology*. DOI: [10.1002/9780470147658.chpsy0311](https://doi.org/10.1002/9780470147658.chpsy0311). This scholarly chapter reviews developmental research in which prosocial behavior can be assessed through observations, reports, and task performance, reinforcing that measurement method and developmental context matter. The reel uses it only for the general measurement distinction and does not make developmental or diagnostic claims.

## Research and safety limits

Prosocial measures depend on the recipient, stakes, instructions, incentives, social relationship, culture, time pressure, and whether the outcome is self-report, observation, a choice, or a later behavior. Empathy, compassion, intention, helping, cooperation, and altruism overlap but are not interchangeable. The reel will avoid diagnosing empathy, judging morality, manipulating viewers to give, or claiming that a single task predicts a person’s future behavior. AI narration and deterministic conceptual visuals will be disclosed.
