# Reel 0060 browser/research findings

## Source 1 — Falzarano & Siedlecki (2019)
DOI: 10.5709/acp-0276-0
URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7256647/
Title: Investigating the Relations Among Different Measures of False Memory.

The article explains that laboratory work uses multiple paradigms sometimes grouped under “false memory”: whole new events, misleading details, DRM critical lures, general recognition, source monitoring, and other tasks. It notes that these measures can show weak or nonexistent correlations, and that the unitary label can hide important methodological differences. DRM uses semantically related lists and an unpresented critical lure; misinformation uses externally supplied misleading post-event information. Safe interpretation: an error in one paradigm is a task-specific outcome and should not automatically be generalized to a person’s overall memory or suggestibility.

## Source 2 — Gatti, Rinaldi, Mazzoni, & Vecchi (2024)
DOI: 10.1038/s41598-023-50687-z
URL: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-50687-z
Title: Semantic and episodic processes differently predict false memories in the DRM task.

The study asked participants to recognize studied words and distinguish them from new words/critical lures in a DRM task, while also assessing semantic memory, episodic memory, and episodic-source memory. It reports that semantic and episodic scores related differently to false recognitions of critical lures, while source-memory score did not show the same effect. Safe interpretation: false recognition, semantic performance, episodic performance, and source memory are separate measured variables; a relationship in one sample and paradigm is not a universal individual prediction.

## Source 3 — Lentoor (2023)
DOI: 10.3934/Neuroscience.2023020
URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10567586/
Title: Cognitive and neural mechanisms underlying false memories: misinformation, distortion or erroneous configuration?

The review defines false memory as distorted remembering or remembering events that did not take place, while emphasizing that errors can involve encoding, consolidation, retrieval, misinformation, or source-monitoring processes. It describes behavioral research and later neuroimaging work as providing preliminary links between observed memory errors and associated neural processes. Safe interpretation: false-memory research measures errors in particular tasks and may associate them with neural signals; it does not prove lying, intent, a stable “false-memory trait,” or direct access to an individual’s memory contents.

## Source 4 — Nichols & Loftus (2019)
DOI: 10.1080/09658211.2019.1611862
URL: https://doi.org/10.1080/09658211.2019.1611862
Title: Who is susceptible in three false memory tasks?

The PubMed record identifies three established paradigms: a misinformation task, the Deese–Roediger–McDermott (DRM) list-learning paradigm, and an imagination-inflation exercise. The study examined relationships among individual-difference and false-memory outcomes across these paradigms, rather than treating a single task as a universal trait measure. Safe interpretation: susceptibility depends on the paradigm and measured outcome; there is no basis for claiming that one task identifies a person who is generally prone or resistant to false memories.
