# Reel 0001 research notes — affect labeling

## Source 1: PubMed PMID 17576282
- Title: “Putting feelings into words: affect labeling disrupts amygdala activity in response to affective stimuli”
- Authors: Matthew D. Lieberman et al.
- Journal/year: Psychological Science, 2007;18(5):421–428.
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01916.x
- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17576282/
- Evidence captured: In an fMRI study, affect labeling compared with other encoding tasks diminished amygdala and other limbic responses to negative emotional images; right ventrolateral prefrontal cortex activity increased; RVLPFC and amygdala activity were inversely correlated, with mediation by medial prefrontal cortex. This supports a cautious “may help regulate emotional reactivity” statement, not a guaranteed calming effect.

## Source 3: Yoshimura, Shimomura & Onoda (2024), BMC Psychology
- Title: “Diminished negative emotion regulation through affect labeling and reappraisal: insights from functional near infrared spectroscopy on lateral prefrontal cortex activation”
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-024-02103-y
- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39482753/
- PMC: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11528986/
- Study details: 35 participants viewed negative-emotion vignettes. Reappraisal reduced subjective negative emotion with and without affect labeling; in this experiment, adding affect labeling before reappraisal was less effective subjectively than reappraisal alone, while increasing bilateral DLPFC and right ventral PFC oxyhemoglobin signals. The authors conclude the interaction is complex and needs further study.
- Editorial implication: Do not claim that naming emotions always improves every regulation strategy. Present affect labeling as a potentially useful observation/naming step, with context and individual variation.

## Source 4: Levy-Gigi & Shamay-Tsoory (2022), PLOS ONE
- Title: “Affect labeling: The role of timing and intensity”
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0279303
- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36580454/
- PMC: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9799301/
- Study details: Experiment 1 included 63 participants and found distress reduction across simultaneous, immediate-subsequent, and 10-second delayed labeling conditions. Experiment 2 included 79 participants and found labeling reduced distress for high-intensity aversive conditions but increased distress in low-intensity conditions.
- Editorial implication: A short practice can be framed as “notice and name what you feel,” but not as universally calming; intensity and timing may matter.

## Source 5: Burklund, Creswell, Irwin & Lieberman (2014), Frontiers in Psychology
- Title: “The common and distinct neural bases of affect labeling and reappraisal in healthy adults”
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00221
- URL: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00221/full
- Study details: 39 healthy older adults; affect labeling and reappraisal showed overlapping prefrontal activity, similar decreases in amygdala activity, and correlated reductions in self-reported distress relative to observe-only controls. The paper describes labeling as incidental rather than intentional regulation.
- Editorial implication: State sample and task context; do not generalize a lab finding into treatment efficacy.

## Source 6: UCLA Health research summary (2007)
