## Source 1 — Bamert & Inauen (2022/2023), Frontiers in Psychology

**Title:** *Physiological stress reactivity and recovery: Some laboratory results transfer to daily life*
**DOI:** 10.3389/fpsyg.2022.943065
**Opened URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9421134/

The review explains that laboratory work commonly uses objective measures such as vagally mediated heart-rate variability (vmHRV) to study stress reactivity and recovery, while laboratory settings offer controlled variation but may use stressors that are less personally relevant than everyday events. It notes that daily-life factors such as alcohol, nicotine, caffeine, breathing pace, loud noise, and body posture can influence vmHRV, and that stressor pile-up can interfere with recovery. It also warns that coarse windows such as 24-hour or daytime-versus-nighttime aggregation can miss the temporal dynamics of reactivity and recovery.

**Bounded use:** Recovery is a time-dependent pattern around an event, not a single universal reading. vmHRV is a physiological correlate influenced by context; it is not a direct readout of a person's mental state or a diagnosis.

## Source 2 — Tramel et al. (2023), Healthcare

**Title:** *An Examination of Subjective and Objective Measures of Stress in Tactical Populations: A Scoping Review*
**DOI:** 10.3390/healthcare11182515
**Opened URL:** https://www.mdpi.com/2227-9032/11/18/2515

The abstract reports a critical review of research on subjective and objective stress outcomes in tactical occupations. It describes 42 retained studies from 17,171 records and says commonly used objective outcomes included heart rate, cortisol, and body temperature, while subjective measures included ratings of perceived exertion and the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory. The review describes these measures as often used in combination to monitor stressors in training and operational contexts. It also discusses shift work and sleep-cycle disruption as contextual factors in some occupational studies.

**Bounded use:** A recovery study may combine self-report and physiological measures, but they represent different constructs. The review concerns tactical populations and stress outcomes; it cannot justify generalizing a particular marker to every person or claiming that one biomarker equals recovery.

## R0079 measurement map so far

| Construct | Example operational measure | What it can indicate | What it cannot establish alone |
|---|---|---|---|
| Subjective state | Self-report of stress, affect, perceived exertion, or anxiety | A participant's reported experience at a time point | The full physiological state or a diagnosis |
| Autonomic physiology | Heart rate or vmHRV | A time-varying cardiovascular/autonomic correlate | A unique mental state or causal mechanism |
| Endocrine physiology | Cortisol | An endocrine response sampled at specified times | A universal measure of psychological recovery |
| Context and timing | Baseline, task, recovery window, daily-life sampling | Change and trajectory around an event | A timeless trait or one-number summary |

## Safety boundaries

The reel must distinguish stressor exposure, self-report, physiological correlates, timing, and outcomes. It must not diagnose, prescribe, promise recovery, infer a person's thoughts directly from a biomarker, or present association as causation. The final script will use cautious wording such as “अध्ययन माप सकता है” and “यह अकेला माप पर्याप्त नहीं है।”

## Source 3 — Zietse et al. (online 2025; issue 2026), Development and Psychopathology

**Title:** *Daily resilience: A systematic review of measures and associations with well-being and mental health in experience sampling studies*
**DOI:** 10.1017/S0954579425000197
**Opened URL:** https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/development-and-psychopathology/article/daily-resilience-a-systematic-review-of-measures-and-associations-with-wellbeing-and-mental-health-in-experience-sampling-studies/DF3BF7ABADBCC46C175FF544F3BB1B4C

The systematic review defines resilience either as a dynamic process of adapting to or recovering from stressors or as a positive outcome of functioning after stress. Its abstract reports 36 included experience-sampling studies. Daily resilience was operationalized either with self-report items adapted from trait measures or as affective recovery after daily stressors. The review explicitly distinguishes a self-reported ability to recover, which reflects subjective coping experience, from recovery of affect, which is intended to capture a dynamic process. It explains that repeated short questionnaires can assess mood, symptoms, behavior, and context in real time; daily diary studies may assess mood, behaviors, and events once per day. The authors also state that static trait questionnaires cannot capture the dynamic recovery process.

**Bounded use:** “Recovery” is not one universal variable. A study's operational definition may be a subjective rating, an affect trajectory, or an outcome of functioning. The review reports associations with variables such as sleep quality and optimism; those associations do not establish that any one factor causes faster recovery or that a person can be classified from one measurement.

## Source 4 — De Calheiros Velozo et al. (2023), Stress and Health

**Title:** *Is daily-life stress reactivity a measure of stress recovery? An investigation of laboratory and daily-life stress*
**DOI:** 10.1002/smi.3213
**Opened URL:** https://research.utwente.nl/en/publications/is-daily-life-stress-reactivity-a-measure-of-stress-recovery-anin/

The University of Twente research record identifies this as a peer-reviewed article. Its abstract says typical laboratory reactivity was calculated as the difference between control and stress, while laboratory recovery was the difference between stress and post-stress. The study compared those measures with event-related daily-life stress reactivity. Fifty-three healthy adults aged 19–35 completed a laboratory session using the repeated Montreal Imaging Stress Task and eight days of experience sampling. Measures included negative affect, heart rate, heart-rate variability, and skin conductance level. The reported findings showed no strong associations between laboratory and daily-life measures except for laboratory affective recovery and daily-life reactivity.

**Bounded use:** A laboratory recovery score and daily-life stress response are not automatically interchangeable. Context, task, sampling schedule, and outcome type matter. This supports the reel's central caution: researchers must state what was measured, when, and under which conditions.

## Refined R0079 evidence map

| Question | Evidence-bounded answer |
|---|---|
| What is the “starting point”? | Researchers may define a baseline, control, stress-period, or post-stress measurement; the comparison must be stated rather than assumed. |
| What can “recovery” mean? | A difference or trajectory in self-reported affect, physiological signals, or functioning after a stressor; definitions vary by design. |
| How is everyday recovery sampled? | Repeated experience-sampling prompts or daily diaries can record feelings, behavior, context, and timing. |
| Can laboratory and everyday recovery be equated? | Not necessarily; one peer-reviewed comparison found little cross-context correspondence except for a specific affective result. |
| What is the safe conclusion? | Measurement choices shape the conclusion. A biomarker, self-report, or one time point is not a direct mind-reader or diagnosis. |
