# Reel 0040 research retrieval audit

## Research question

Reel 0040 asks: **When researchers say that changing the environment or adding/removing friction affects behavior, what did they actually measure?** The angle is methodological, not prescriptive. It distinguishes physical/choice-architecture changes, context stability, self-reported automaticity, observed behavior, and group-level estimates.

## Evidence notes

Stojanovic et al. define context as the combination of physical environment and the point in the flow of the day. In Study 1, 95 university students contributed 2,482 habit repetitions over six weeks; one group was instructed to keep place and time stable and the other to vary them. In Study 2, the authors analyzed 2,368 habit repetitions from 308 habits and 218 users of a habit-building app without manipulating context. In both datasets, measured context stability predicted more automaticity and higher habit-repetition goal attainment, with automaticity partially mediating the latter association. The paper measures self-reported automaticity, perceived context stability, and goal attainment after repetitions; it does not read a person’s mind or establish a universal habit-formation timeline.

The same paper reports that habit research often treats context as a cue and that changing contexts can disrupt habitual behavior. It also describes how context includes both place and time. Its conclusions support a conditional statement that stable contexts can be associated with smoother or more automatic execution in these datasets; they do not justify telling every individual to use one fixed environment or promising a particular outcome.

Mertens et al. classify **change option-related effort** under decision-structure interventions: physical or financial effort can be adjusted to remove friction from a desirable option. Their meta-analysis covered more than 200 studies, 447 effect sizes from 212 publications, and 2,148,439 participants/observations as reported in the article. The pooled estimate was Cohen’s d = 0.43 with a 95% CI of [0.38, 0.48], described as small-to-medium. The effect sizes were highly heterogeneous (I² reported as 99.67%); the article’s prediction interval included effects that could be negative, and the authors estimated that approximately 15% of interventions might backfire. The authors also reported moderate publication bias; sensitivity analyses attenuated the pooled estimate under assumed bias. Therefore, the reel must say “average effects across studies” rather than “friction always works.”

Verplanken and Whitmarsh review habits as memory-based responses to cues acquired through repetition in stable contexts. They argue that context changes, incentives, legislation, nudges, implementation intentions, and life transitions can disrupt existing routines, while stable performance contexts can help desired routines endure. This is a review-level synthesis, not a universal law. It supports the visual distinction between a cue/context change and a direct measure of behavior.

## Evidence boundary for the script

The reel may say: “Environmental friction is usually operationalized as a change in effort, access, arrangement, defaults, or context—not as a direct measurement of willpower or an invisible brain state.” It may say: “Researchers then record an outcome such as a choice, completion, repetition, response time, self-report scale, or goal-attainment report.” It must avoid: a guaranteed behavior-change claim, a diagnosis, a prescription, a fixed time-to-habit claim, a claim that friction works equally across people or domains, or a claim that a questionnaire is objective automaticity.
