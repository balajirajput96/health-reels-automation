# Reel 0031 — behavior change: studies actually measure what?

## Evidence question

This reel explains how behavior-change research measures an outcome without confusing intention, self-report, initial response, adherence, and durable maintenance. It also separates efficacy under controlled conditions from effectiveness in real-world settings and from the mechanisms or implementation process.

## Vetted evidence map

| Evidence point | What the source supports | Boundary for narration |
|---|---|---|
| Behavior-change evaluation has multiple questions | Evaluation can ask whether an intervention works under specified conditions, works in practice, how it works, and how context changes the result | Do not collapse all questions into one “works/doesn’t work” label |
| Intention is not behavior | Attitudes and intentions can relate to later behavior, but the relationship is incomplete and experimental intention changes may produce smaller behavior changes | A stated plan is not proof of action or maintenance |
| Outcomes can be objective or reported | Researchers may use observation, records, device measures, task performance, self-report, or a combination | Self-report and objective measures answer different questions |
| Initial change and maintenance differ | Adherence and long-term maintenance can decline after an initial response, and follow-up duration affects interpretation | Short-term improvement is not a guarantee of durable change |
| Context and implementation matter | Population, setting, delivery, resources, barriers, and implementation can alter observed effectiveness | Average results do not guarantee individual benefit |

## Vetted scholarly sources

1. Matthews, L., & Simpson, S. A. (2020). “Evaluation of Behavior Change Interventions.” In *The Handbook of Behavior Change*, pp. 318–332. Cambridge University Press. DOI: [10.1017/9781108677318.022](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781108677318.022). The chapter distinguishes efficacy, real-world effectiveness, mechanisms, and context, and discusses experimental, quasi-experimental, and nonexperimental evaluations. It emphasizes selecting relevant outcomes and considering implementation and wider context.

2. Conner, M., & Norman, P. (2026). “Attitudes, Intentions, and Behavior Change.” *Annual Review of Psychology*, 77, 311–337. DOI: [10.1146/annurev-psych-013125-042110](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-013125-042110). This review reports that attitudes and intentions have small-to-medium relationships with behavior change, while experimental changes in attitudes and intentions produce smaller changes in behavior. The reel uses the intention–behavior gap as a measurement distinction, not an individual prediction.

3. Middleton, K. R., Anton, S. D., & Perri, M. G. (2013). “Long-Term Adherence to Health Behavior Change.” *American Journal of Lifestyle Medicine*, 7(6), 395–404. DOI: [10.1177/1559827613488867](https://doi.org/10.1177/1559827613488867). This review describes the challenge of sustaining lifestyle behavior change and distinguishes initial responses, adherence, relapse, and maintenance over time. The reel uses only the general evaluation distinction and gives no personal health advice.

4. Michie, S., Atkins, L., & West, R. (2018). “The Behaviour Change Wheel” methods framework as applied in behavior-change-technique evaluation. The relevant peer-reviewed evaluation source was identified in the research search, but its PMC page returned a reCAPTCHA during retrieval. No claim requiring inaccessible full text is used in the narration. The reel relies on the openly extracted Cambridge and Annual Review material plus the accessible adherence review.

## Research and safety limits

Behavior-change results depend on the target behavior, measurement method, time horizon, population, setting, intervention fidelity, and implementation conditions. A self-reported intention is not the same as observed behavior; a short-term outcome is not long-term maintenance; and a group average is not an individual forecast. The reel will avoid prescribing health actions, promising guaranteed change, using manipulative persuasion, diagnosing motivation, or claiming that an intervention works for everyone. AI narration and deterministic conceptual visuals will be disclosed.
