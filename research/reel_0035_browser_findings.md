

## Source-level retrieval findings

Faul and LaBar (2023), *Psychological Review*, DOI 10.1037/rev0000394, PubMed PMID 36201828, was opened from the official PubMed page. The abstract defines mood-congruent memory as a bias in emotional memory toward content congruent with a past or current mood. The review notes that studies may observe enhanced accuracy for previously encoded mood-congruent content, preferential recall of mood-congruent autobiographical events, or false memory for mood-congruent lures. It also discusses mood-incongruent recall that may support mood repair, methodological conditions that shape effects, limited neuroimaging research, and the need for more attention to consolidation. The reel will present this as a review of conditions and possible patterns, not as a universal rule or a diagnostic explanation.

The official PMC page https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10076454/ was also opened but presented a reCAPTCHA interstitial in the sandbox. The PubMed abstract and DOI record remain the authoritative accessible citation for the final source document.


## Additional cross-checks

Mayer, McCormick, and Strong (1995), *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*, DOI 10.1177/0146167295217008, was opened through the publisher page. Its abstract reports three studies with normal individuals in everyday moods and a total N=614, using category-retrieval and word-association style tasks. The article also documents task-reliability concerns in a smaller item version. The reel will use this source to show that memory results depend on the task and measurement reliability, not to claim that mood always determines recall.

Matt, Vázquez, and Campbell (1992), *Clinical Psychology Review*, DOI 10.1016/0272-7358(92)90116-P, was opened through ScienceDirect. The meta-analytic review examined mood-congruent recall across normal, subclinical, clinical, induced-depressed, and induced-elated groups, and considered self-referenced encoding and mood intensity. The abstract reports different patterns across groups and notes caveats. This supports describing mood-congruent recall as a measured, context-sensitive pattern rather than a universal memory law.
