## Verified source record

### [1] Rosenkranz, Takano, Watkins & Ehring (2020), *Assessing repetitive negative thinking in daily life: Development of an ecological momentary assessment paradigm*

- DOI: `10.1371/journal.pone.0231783`
- URL: https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0231783
- Open full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7170251/
- Evidence type: peer-reviewed methods and validation study.
- Relevant boundary: repetitive negative thinking is commonly assessed with trait questionnaires, but retrospective or global reports may be influenced by memory, beliefs, and current state. The study developed an ecological momentary assessment paradigm with repeated smartphone prompts and evaluated process-focused and content-focused items. Momentary sampling can capture context and fluctuations, but it also creates participant burden and still relies on self-report.

### [2] Hoebeke, Blanchard, Contreras & Heeren (2022), *An Experience Sampling Measure of the Key Features of Rumination*

- DOI: `10.36131/cnfioritieditore20220504`
- URL: https://doi.org/10.36131/cnfioritieditore20220504
- Open full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9597648/
- Evidence type: peer-reviewed experience-sampling instrument development and validation study.
- Relevant boundary: rumination can be treated as a dynamic, multifaceted process rather than a single unitary score. The study assessed five features—perseveration, brooding, negativity, self-criticism, and replaying—through brief repeated reports in everyday life, while noting that further validation is warranted. A momentary score is a report about a sampled episode, not a complete description of a person.

### [3] Kornacka, Buczny & Layton (2016), *Assessing Repetitive Negative Thinking Using Categorical and Transdiagnostic Approaches: A Comparison and Validation of Three Polish Language Adaptations of Self-Report Questionnaires*

- DOI: `10.3389/fpsyg.2016.00322`
- URL: https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.00322
- Open full text: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.00322/full
- Evidence type: peer-reviewed methods and questionnaire-comparison study.
- Relevant boundary: the Ruminative Response Scale, Perseverative Thinking Questionnaire, and Mini-Cambridge Exeter Repetitive Thought Scale represent related but non-identical theoretical approaches. They differ in whether they emphasize depressive rumination, content-independent repetitive negative thinking, or processing modes such as abstract-analytic versus concrete-experiential thinking. A score depends on the construct and wording chosen.

## Evidence map for the reel

| Research window | Typical observable | What it can support | What it cannot establish alone |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Trait questionnaire | Typical tendency or usual response to negative mood/problems | A self-reported individual-difference estimate under that scale | A precise count of thoughts or a diagnosis |
| Momentary EMA/ESM | Repeated reports after prompts in daily life | Fluctuations, context, and within-person patterns | A complete record of every thought or an absence of reporting bias |
| Content items | Thinking about feelings, problems, upsetting memories, or future situations | Which reported content is present in a sampled moment | That content alone proves a single mechanism |
| Process items | Repetitiveness, intrusiveness, difficulty disengaging, burden, or duration | Whether sampled thinking has selected process features | That the person is clinically ill or that the process has one cause |
| Questionnaire comparison | Scores from different scales and subscales | How operational definitions differ | That two scales are interchangeable |
| Physiological or neural correlate | A body or brain signal collected during a defined task | Association with that task and report | Literal access to private thoughts or a standalone diagnosis |

## Source and safety boundary

The reel uses “rumination” as an operational research construct, not as a label for the viewer. It distinguishes depressive rumination, broader repetitive negative thinking, and momentary sampled reports. It does not interpret a viewer’s score, infer a mental disorder, promise a treatment outcome, or suggest that a smartphone prompt can read thoughts.
