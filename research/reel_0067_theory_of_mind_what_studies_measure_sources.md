# R0067 Evidence Record: Theory of Mind — What Studies Measure

## Research question and unique angle

This reel asks a narrow question: when a study says it measured “theory of mind,” what was actually observed? The angle is measurement literacy, not a test or diagnosis. The reel contrasts an observable task response with the larger construct researchers are trying to study.

## Evidence table

| Evidence class | What a study may present | What is directly observed | What cannot be claimed from one result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Task behavior | False-belief story, belief/knowledge question, intention or emotion vignette | Choice, verbal answer, explanation, accuracy, and sometimes response time | A complete reading of a participant’s private thoughts or a universal ToM ability |
| Construct coverage | Tasks about beliefs, knowledge, desires, intentions, emotions, percepts, or non-literal communication | Performance on the selected sub-ability | That one task covers every ToM component |
| Measurement quality | Scores across multiple items or tasks, reliability/validity analyses | Consistency and relation to the intended construct, when reported | That a high score is automatically stable across contexts or predicts all social behavior |
| Confounded task demands | Language, memory, executive demands, outcome knowledge, story format | Performance under the exact information and instructions given | That an incorrect answer uniquely means absent ToM |

## Source 1 — systematic review of young-child ToM measures

Beaudoin, C., Leblanc, E., Gagner, C., & Beauchamp, M. H. (2020). “Systematic Review and Inventory of Theory of Mind Measures for Young Children.” *Frontiers in Psychology*, 10, 2905. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02905

The review defines ToM as inferring mental states of self and others, including desires, knowledge, beliefs, and intentions, to predict behavior. Across 830 studies it identified 220 measures for ages 0–5, covering seven categories: emotions, desires, intentions, percepts, knowledge, beliefs, and mentalistic understanding of non-literal communication. The review highlights limited coverage of sub-abilities, lack of standardization, and limited psychometric information. This supports the claim that ToM is a family of abilities and that the exact measure matters.

## Source 2 — classic false-belief task confound

Ghrear, S., Baimel, A., Haddock, T., & Birch, S. A. J. (2021). “Are the classic false belief tasks cursed? Young children are just as likely as older children to pass a false belief task when they are not required to overcome the curse of knowledge.” *PLOS ONE*, 16(2), e0244141. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0244141

In a Sally–Anne-style task, a protagonist hides an object, leaves, and another character moves it. Participants predict where the protagonist will look, which requires tracking the protagonist’s belief rather than the object’s actual location. The study compared trials where participants were told the exact new location with trials where they knew only that the object moved. Children were more accurate when the destination was not disclosed, consistent with the possibility that outcome knowledge can interfere with false-belief reasoning. The safe conclusion is about a task-design effect, not a diagnosis of ToM.

## Source 3 — adult individual-difference measurement review

Yeung, K., Apperly, I., & Devine, R. T. (2024). “Measures of individual differences in adult theory of mind: A systematic review.” *Neuroscience and Biobehavioral Reviews*, 157, 105481. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2023.105481

This systematic review identified 75 measures in 273 studies of individual differences in adults’ ToM. It reports prevalent ceiling effects, limited evidence for reliability and validity because psychometric properties were often not reported, and inconsistent interrelations among measures. The authors call for broader psychometric evidence and more specific hypotheses about links between ToM and social outcomes. This supports a cautious ending: a single score should not be treated as a definitive, stable measure of a person’s overall social understanding.

## Evidence-bounded conclusion

The strongest defensible claim is: ToM studies measure performance on selected tasks designed to probe specific mental-state inferences. The observed data may include answers, accuracy, explanations, or timing, depending on the design. Interpretation requires attention to the targeted sub-ability, language and memory demands, information available to the participant, scoring, and psychometric evidence. The sources do not support direct mind-reading, individual diagnosis, or a guarantee that one task score generalizes to every real-world social situation.
