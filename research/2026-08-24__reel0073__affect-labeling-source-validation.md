# REEL-0073 Source Validation: Naming Emotions as a Reflection Skill

## Editorial question

Can a reel explain affect labeling—the act of putting a feeling into words—without turning one neuroimaging study into a assuranced calming technique or mental-health management?

## Source correction

The selector’s secondary URL had a malformed DOI path. The verified paper is **Lieberman et al. (2007), “Putting feelings into words: affect labeling disrupts amygdala activity in response to affective stimuli,” Psychological Science 18(5):421–428**, DOI `10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01916.x`, PMID 17576282 [2].

## Claim register

| Claim | Evidence class | Support | Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Putting a feeling into words is commonly called affect labeling. | Peer-reviewed study / institutional explanation | Lieberman et al. studied “putting feelings into words” as affect labeling; UCLA describes the task as choosing an emotion word for an affective face [1] [2]. | The laboratory task is not identical to every journaling or therapy exercise. |
| In a small fMRI study, affect labeling was associated with lower amygdala and other limbic responses to negative emotional images than comparison encoding tasks. | Small laboratory neuroimaging study | The PubMed abstract reports diminished amygdala and other limbic responses during affect labeling [2]. UCLA reports 30 adults aged 18–36 participated in the study [1]. | This is an association in a specific laboratory task, not proof that naming any personal feeling will calm everyone or address distress. |
| Affect labeling increased activity in the right ventrolateral prefrontal cortex, and the activity pattern was consistent with a possible pathway involving medial prefrontal cortex and amygdala. | Mechanistic hypothesis supported by one study | Lieberman et al. reported increased RVLPFC activity and an inverse relationship with amygdala activity mediated by MPFC [2]. | Say “the study suggests” or “was consistent with,” not “this is how the brain definitely calms itself.” |
| Pausing to use a specific feeling word can be a reasonable reflection cue. | Practical synthesis | The study and UCLA explanation provide a rationale for trying a simple labeling cue [1] [2]. | This is not a prescription, clinical assessment, or substitute for therapy. It may not feel helpful in every situation. |
| Persistent or severe distress may require professional support. | Safety boundary | The reel’s health context requires a clear limit between an educational reflection cue and mental-health care. | Do not advise viewers to handle crisis, trauma, or severe symptoms with labeling alone. |

## Editorial boundaries

The reel will use cautious language: “one small fMRI study found,” “the task was specific,” and “this does not assurance calm.” The practical cue will be to pause and name the feeling with a tentative phrase such as “I’m noticing anger” or “I’m feeling overwhelmed,” without insisting that the label be perfect.

The reel will not claim that naming emotions shuts off the amygdala, prevents anxiety, resolutions depression, improves relationships, or replaces professional care. It will not present the finding as a Buddhist teaching or as a universal neuroscience law.
