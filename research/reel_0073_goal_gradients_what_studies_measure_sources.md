# R0073 Evidence Record: Goal Gradients — What Studies Measure

## Research question and unique angle

This reel asks what goal-gradient studies actually measure when they report greater effort near a goal. The angle is to distinguish observable behavior and protocol-specific self-report from a private motivational state, and to show that context can moderate the pattern.

## Evidence table

| Measurement class | Example operationalization | Direct outcome | Boundary on interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Purchase behavior | Purchases in a café reward program as the free reward gets closer | Purchase frequency | A behavior within a specified reward program, not a universal motivation meter |
| Online task effort | Site visits, songs rated per visit, and persistence while approaching a certificate reward | Visits, ratings, persistence | Incentive, task, and population are protocol-bound |
| Completion behavior | Time to complete required purchases, including a card with apparent bonus progress | Completion speed | Illusory progress changes perceived distance in that program; it does not prove a general law |
| Experimental motivation/performance | Goal proximity manipulated with experienced power varied | Self-reported motivation and task performance | The pattern can differ by moderator; high power reduced the proximity effect in one study |
| Retention/re-engagement | Later program retention and faster re-engagement predicted from acceleration | Return behavior | An association within a reward-program context, not direct evidence of private intent |

## Source 1 — foundational human field and laboratory evidence

Kivetz, R., Urminsky, O., & Zheng, Y. (2006). “The Goal-Gradient Hypothesis Resurrected: Purchase Acceleration, Illusionary Goal Progress, and Customer Retention.” *Journal of Marketing Research*, 43(1), 39–58. https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkr.43.1.39

The paper used field experiments, secondary customer data, paper-and-pencil problems, and Tobit/logit models. Its abstract reports more frequent café purchases closer to a free coffee; more visits, songs rated per visit, and persistence closer to an online reward; faster completion when a loyalty card appeared to contain two preexisting bonus stamps; and a relationship between acceleration, retention, and re-engagement in the program. Safe conclusion: these are observed purchase, visit, rating, persistence, completion, retention, and re-engagement outcomes under specified incentive systems.

## Source 2 — boundary condition

Schmid, P. C. (2020). “Power reduces the goal gradient effect.” *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*, 90, 104003. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2020.104003. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022103119306705

The abstract reports that low-power participants were less motivated when goal attainment seemed far away rather than close, whereas high-power participants’ motivation was less affected by goal proximity. It also reports different performance patterns by power condition and concludes that experienced power is a boundary condition. Safe conclusion: goal proximity effects depend on experimental context and moderators; they are not automatic for every person or goal.

## Source 3 — progress markers and locomotion

Van den Bergh, B., Heuvinck, N., Schellekens, G. A. C., & Vermeir, I. (2016). “Altering Speed of Locomotion.” *Journal of Consumer Research*, 43(3), 407–428. https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucw031

The Erasmus University Rotterdam record identifies this as a peer-reviewed article reporting lab and field experiments. Progress markers along a path changed perceived goal progress and walking speed: customers walked faster when fewer markers were placed along the path. The effect diminished when markers were unrelated to the goal and reversed when markers were more salient than the goal. Safe conclusion: the measured outcome was walking speed in a retail setting, and marker meaning/salience moderated the pattern.

## Evidence-bounded conclusion

Goal-gradient research often measures observable effort, persistence, purchases, visits, task completion, or self-reported motivation while perceived goal distance changes. The pattern is context-sensitive: apparent progress and reward design matter, and experienced power can reduce or eliminate the effect in one experimental setting. A progress bar or near-finish feeling should not be presented as guaranteed motivation, and observed behavior should not be treated as direct mind-reading.
