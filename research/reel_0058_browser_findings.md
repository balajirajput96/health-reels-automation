# Reel 0058 browser/research findings

## Source 1 — Dikmen et al. (2014)
DOI: 10.1017/S1355617714000460
URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4254833/
Title: Measuring Episodic Memory Across the Lifespan: NIH Toolbox Picture Sequence Memory Test.

The accessible article describes the Picture Sequence Memory Test: pictured objects and activities are presented in a fixed order and verbally described; the participant later reproduces the sequence over three learning trials. The reported outputs include test–retest reliability, construct validity, and performance relationships to established episodic-memory measures. Safe interpretation: this is performance on a particular picture-sequence procedure, not a complete measure of every aspect of episodic memory or everyday remembering.

## Source 2 — Kunčická et al. (2024)
DOI: 10.1523/ENEURO.0073-24.2024
URL: https://www.eneuro.org/content/11/8/ENEURO.0073-24.2024
Title: Neuroscientist’s Behavioral Toolbox for Studying Episodic-Like Memory.

The page returned article text despite a browser captcha. Its abstract and introduction state that different tasks model different aspects, including what–where–when or what–where content, source memory, free recall, temporal binding, and retrieval dynamics. The review explicitly cautions that no single task fully assesses episodic memory and that alternative explanations can account for behavior; appropriate control groups and tests are needed. No claim about animal consciousness or human diagnosis is used.

## Evidence boundary

Recall, recognition, source/context memory, sequence reproduction, response accuracy, response time, and confidence are task outputs or reports. Neural signals and model parameters are proxies or estimates tied to a task and analysis. No single task is treated as a complete readout of autobiographical memory, mental time travel, or a person’s general cognitive health.

## Source 3 — Windsor, Dering, & Donaldson (2026)
DOI: 10.5334/joc.477
URL: https://journalofcognition.org/articles/10.5334/joc.477
Title: Does the Experience of Remembering Differentially Influence the Factual Accuracy of Recognition, and Confidence in Its Accuracy?

The open article reports two studies: an item-recognition task using similar photographic lures and a source-memory task using picture-location associations. It separately analyzed recognition accuracy, source accuracy, the subjective experience of remembering, and confidence. The abstract states that high confidence did not always imply high factual accuracy, and vivid memories could be confidently recollected yet factually inaccurate. Safe interpretation: confidence and subjective vividness are metacognitive reports/experiences, not guarantees of accuracy.

## Source 4 — Guo, Shubeck, & Hu (2021)
DOI: 10.3389/fpsyg.2021.691577
URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8511408/
Title: Relationship Between Item and Source Memory: Explanation of Connection-Strength Model.

The open review describes the controversy over whether item memory and source memory are positively related, independent, or negatively related across designs. It emphasizes that experimental design determines which item–source connection is encoded and retrieved, and it uses empirical, cognitive-neuroscience, and mathematical-modeling evidence. Safe interpretation: item recognition and source/context accuracy are related measures whose relationship depends on task connections; one should not be treated as a universal proxy for the other.
