# Reel 0043 research retrieval audit

## Research question

Reel 0043 asks: **When a meditation-training study says “dose” or “progress,” what did researchers actually count?** The safe angle is to separate assigned practice duration, self-reported adherence, questionnaire outcomes, behavioral or EEG outcomes, dropout, and study-specific analyses. Practice minutes are not automatically the same thing as meditation skill or a guaranteed benefit.

## Shang et al. (2023) checked details

The original research article studied EEG measurements from 11 novice MBSR practitioners, listed as six men and five women, with mean age 35.7 years. EEG was recorded during resting and meditation at early and late training stages. The analysis used deep-learning and traditional machine-learning classifiers with different inter-subject, mixed-subject, intra-subject, and subject-transfer strategies. The reported classification accuracies are outputs of those specific models and validation strategies; they are not a direct meter of meditation quality, attention, or a viewer’s private mental state.

The paper’s conclusion is that short-term meditation training had EEG-recognizable state and trait effects in the study’s data. Because the sample was 11 people and the claims depend on model features, classifier design, and validation strategy, the reel will avoid presenting the reported percentages as universal effects or individual prediction. EEG patterns are treated as model-derived physiological correlates.

## Fincham et al. (2023) checked details

The online trial randomly allocated participants to four mindfulness meditation interventions and asked them to practice daily for two weeks. Two conditions used sitting meditation of approximately 9.8 or 29.2 minutes daily; two used mindful movement of approximately 8.9 or 30.1 minutes daily. The primary outcome was the 14-item Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Well-Being Scale. Secondary outcomes included GP-CORE and the Mindful Attention Awareness Scale. Adherence was collected by asking how many of the 14 assigned home sessions participants practiced, so adherence was a retrospective self-report rather than a device-recorded minute count. Dropout was evaluated through post-intervention questionnaire completion.

The study’s analysis considered pre-versus-post change and used dose, type, and time factors, along with self-reported adherence as a covariate. The article explicitly notes that the MAAS is focused on present-moment attention and awareness but is composed of negatively keyed items about mindlessness, and that adherence was self-reported. The reel uses these details to show that “dose” can mean assigned minutes while “adherence” can mean reported sessions, and that outcome scales are separate from practice exposure.

## Evidence boundaries

The reel may say that researchers can count assigned practice time, completed sessions, self-reported adherence, questionnaire scores, dropout, behavioral tasks, or EEG features. It may say that randomization improves causal inference for the assigned intervention relative to a comparison, but a two-week online trial or an 11-person EEG classification study remains bounded by its sample, protocol, outcome, and analysis.

The reel must not say that more minutes always produce more benefit, that EEG proves meditation skill, that a classifier reads thoughts, or that a study diagnoses a person. No mental-health diagnosis, treatment recommendation, prescription, individualized meditation plan, guaranteed outcome, or universal timeline will be given.
