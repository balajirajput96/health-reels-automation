# R0074 Evidence Record: Expectancy–Value — What Studies Measure

## Research question and unique angle

This reel asks what expectancy–value studies actually measure when they discuss motivation. The angle is to separate task-specific beliefs and values from observed choice, persistence, engagement, and performance, while keeping self-report and outcome measures distinct.

## Evidence table

| Construct or outcome | Typical operationalization | What it can indicate | Boundary on interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Expectancy for success | A person’s belief about how well they can complete an upcoming or specific task | Task-specific perceived likelihood or competence belief | A belief report, not proof of ability or a guaranteed result |
| Attainment value | Perceived personal importance or identity relevance of the task | Why the task matters to that person | Subjective and task-specific; not a universal value ranking |
| Intrinsic value | Expected enjoyment, interest, or curiosity in the task | Anticipated enjoyment or interest | Self-report about a particular activity, not a permanent trait |
| Utility value | Perceived usefulness for present or future goals | Perceived relevance or usefulness | Context- and goal-dependent; does not guarantee effort |
| Cost | Effort, opportunity, or emotional burden perceived in choosing the task | What the person expects to give up or endure | Often distinct from value; not merely financial cost |
| Behavioral outcomes | Activity choice, participation, persistence, engagement, completion, or performance | Observable or recorded task outcome | A relationship in a defined setting, not direct mind-reading |

## Source 1 — foundational model

Wigfield, A., & Eccles, J. S. (2000). “Expectancy–Value Theory of Achievement Motivation.” *Contemporary Educational Psychology*, 25(1), 68–81. https://doi.org/10.1006/ceps.1999.1015

The abstract states that the article defines ability beliefs, expectancies for success, and components of subjective task values; compares them with self-efficacy, intrinsic/extrinsic motivation, and interest; and reviews relations of ability-expectancy beliefs and subjective task values to performance and activity choice. Safe conclusion: the model organizes task-specific beliefs and values and examines their relations with choice and performance.

## Source 2 — recent conceptual review

Wang, Q., & Xue, M. (2022). “The implications of expectancy-value theory of motivation in language education.” *Frontiers in Psychology*, 13, 992372. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.992372

The review describes expectancies as predictions or beliefs about dealing with a prospective task, often assessed with questionnaires about perceived ability or self-efficacy. It describes subjective task value as personal perceptions including intrinsic/interest, utility, attainment, and cost. It links these constructs to task choice, attempts, persistence, engagement, participation, and achievement in educational contexts. Safe conclusion: these are belief/value measures connected to outcomes in specified settings, not a direct measure of an overall inner motivation quantity.

## Source 3 — meta-analysis and construct definitions

Shang, C., Moss, A. C., & Chen, A. (2022). “The expectancy-value theory: A meta-analysis of its application in physical education.” *Journal of Sport and Health Science*, 11(1), 52–64. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jshs.2022.01.003. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9923428/

The meta-analysis describes expectancy beliefs for success and subjective task values as related dimensions. It defines attainment value as perceived personal importance, intrinsic value as anticipated enjoyment or interest, utility value as perceived future usefulness, and cost as effort, opportunity, and emotional costs. Safe conclusion: expectancy, value, and cost are distinct constructs that studies operationalize through task-specific beliefs and reported reasons, then examine in relation to motivation or educational outcomes.

## Evidence-bounded conclusion

Expectancy–value studies commonly ask “Can I succeed?” and “Is this task important, enjoyable, useful, or costly?” They may then examine choice, persistence, engagement, or performance in a defined task, subject, or population. Because many constructs are self-reported and outcomes are context-bound, the framework should not be narrated as diagnosis, certain prediction, or proof that someone will inevitably act in one way.
