# Reel 0054 retrieval audit: base-rate reasoning and what studies measure

## Retrieval scope

The retrieval focused on base-rate neglect, Bayesian updating, diagnosticity, task format, individual differences, and the distinction between laboratory single-question tasks and sequential real-world decisions.

## Verified findings

**Pennycook, Trippas, Handley, and Thompson (2014), Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition, DOI 10.1037/a0034887.** The study tested whether base-rate information is necessarily effortful while stereotype-like diagnostic information is intuitive. Across experiments, participants answered problems under belief/statistics instructions and under free-time or time-limited conditions. In conflict problems, base rates interfered with belief judgments to a similar extent as stereotypes interfered with statistics judgments, indexed by increased response time and reduced confidence. The authors concluded that base rates are often underweighted but may be accessible to intuitive processing rather than requiring only effortful reasoning.

**Link and Raab (2021), Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, DOI 10.3758/s13423-021-02024-6.** The researchers analyzed sequential real-world beach-volleyball decisions from 1,347 matches involving expert players. They operationalized base rate as a player's prior success rate over a defined time window and selection rate as how often that player was selected to receive a serve. They found that expert choices were sensitive to base rates and adapted to opponent performance, with a threshold-like change in strategy. The study argues that laboratory single-question findings should not automatically be generalized to real-world sequential expertise.

**Stengård, Juslin, Hahn, and van den Berg (2022), Cognition, DOI 10.1016/j.cognition.2022.105160.** The study systematically varied base rate, hit rate, and false-alarm rate over a broader problem space and compared normalized versus natural-frequency and symbolic versus pictorial presentations. It found that patterns associated with base-rate neglect generalized across a larger space, but there were large individual differences. Model results suggested a bimodal distribution: one group largely ignored base rates while another largely accounted for them with Bayesian-like behavior. The article emphasizes that the apparent effect can depend on task structure, information format, and the model used to describe responses.

**Howe, Perfors, Walker, Kashima, and Fay (2022/2023), Judgment and Decision Making, DOI 10.1017/S1930297500009281.** Across three experiments, the researchers elicited full prior and posterior probability distributions rather than single point estimates. They reported substantial individual variation in base-rate neglect and conservatism, with most participants tending to disregard the base rate less when the prior was made explicit. The method shows why a single answer may not capture the full distribution of a participant's beliefs or cleanly separate prior underweighting from weak likelihood updating.

## Evidence boundaries

Base-rate neglect is an operational pattern: a response underweights prior probability relative to a specified normative model. It is not a diagnosis of intelligence, personality, or an individual's general reasoning ability. Base-rate use can vary with whether information is given as percentages or natural frequencies, whether a causal relationship is made explicit, whether feedback or training is provided, the range of base-rate values, diagnosticity of the cue, time limits, response format, expertise, and whether the task is a single hypothetical question or a sequential real-world decision.

A laboratory answer to a medical-diagnosis-style problem is not itself a clinical diagnosis or an estimate of a person's actual medical risk. The reel is public education only and gives no medical, financial, or individualized decision advice.
