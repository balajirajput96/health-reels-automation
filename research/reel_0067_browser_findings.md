# R0067 Browser Research Findings: Theory of Mind — What Studies Measure

## Source 1: Beaudoin et al. systematic review (Frontiers in Psychology, 2020)
URL: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02905/full

The review defines theory of mind (ToM) as the ability to infer mental states of self and others, including desires, knowledge, beliefs, and intentions, in order to predict behavior. Its inventory of measures for children aged 0–5 identified 220 measures in 830 studies spanning seven categories of mental states/social situations: emotions, desires, intentions, percepts, knowledge, beliefs, and mentalistic understanding of non-literal communication. The review emphasizes that ToM is not one single test or one unitary skill. Measures differ in presentation mode, item count, scoring, target population, and sub-ability. It also reports methodological and psychometric challenges, including limited coverage of sub-abilities, lack of standardization, and sparse psychometric information.

Safe interpretation for the reel: a ToM task provides a task-specific behavioral outcome—often an answer or choice that indicates whether the participant tracked another person’s mental state. It does not by itself prove a broad, stable “mind-reading power,” and results depend on the exact sub-ability, wording, language, task demands, and scoring.

## Source 2: Ghrear, Baimel, Haddock, & Birch, PLOS ONE (2021), “Are the classic false belief tasks cursed?”
URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7894954/

The classic Sally–Anne false-belief task presents a protagonist who hides an object and leaves. Another character moves it while the protagonist is absent. The participant is asked where the protagonist will look. A correct answer requires predicting the protagonist’s belief based on what that protagonist saw, rather than simply reporting the object’s actual location.

The paper specifically tests a possible confound: in the standard version, the participant is told exactly where the object was moved, creating outcome knowledge that the protagonist lacks. In versions where participants knew that the object moved but were not told the destination, children performed more accurately. The authors interpret this pattern as evidence that the classic task can be affected by a “curse of knowledge” bias: participants’ own knowledge of the outcome can interfere with tracking the protagonist’s naïve perspective.

Safe interpretation for the reel: false-belief accuracy is an observable response under particular information and story conditions. A wrong answer can reflect multiple task demands or a knowledge-conflict confound; it should not automatically be treated as absence of ToM.

## Working evidence boundaries

Established: ToM research uses tasks involving beliefs, knowledge, desires, intentions, emotions, and non-literal communication; false-belief tasks ask participants to predict a protagonist’s belief/choice; task performance can be influenced by task design and outcome knowledge.

Not established by these sources: that one score captures all social understanding, that a single task diagnoses a person, or that performance directly reads private thoughts. Neural activation, self-report, and social behavior—if used in a study—would be different measurement classes and should not be conflated with the task answer.
