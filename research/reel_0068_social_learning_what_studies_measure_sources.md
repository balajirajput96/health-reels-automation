# R0068 Evidence Record: Social Learning — What Studies Measure

## Research question and unique angle

This reel asks what a social-learning experiment actually records after someone watches, interacts with, or receives information from another person. The angle is to separate observable task outcomes—such as imitation, emulation, attention, retention, and transfer—from the broad idea of “learning from people.”

## Evidence table

| Evidence class | What a study may manipulate or present | Directly observed outcome | What cannot be concluded from one outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| Social exposure | A model, demonstration, advice, gaze cue, or interaction | The participant’s later choice, action, attention, or response | That every later change was caused only by observation |
| Imitation | A model performs a goal-directed action sequence | Reproduction of the action sequence and possibly the goal | That copying proves full understanding of the model’s intention |
| Emulation | A model reaches an outcome or demonstrates a useful object | The learner reaches a similar outcome, possibly with different actions | That the learner copied the model’s exact method |
| Retention/transfer | A delay, changed object, or novel context after observation | Performance later or in a new situation | That success transfers to all real-world settings |

## Source 1 — observational learning review

Fryling, M. J., Johnston, C., & Hayes, L. J. (2011). “Understanding Observational Learning: An Interbehavioral Approach.” *The Analysis of Verbal Behavior*, 27, 191–203. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF03393102

This open-access review describes observational learning as a complex area in which behavior change after observation is studied. It notes that later reproduction of an observed response can be one way to assess learning from observation, while also discussing different explanatory routes such as generalized imitation, conditioned reinforcement, and rule-governed behavior. The safe interpretation is that a later copied response is an observable outcome; it does not uniquely identify the underlying process.

## Source 2 — developmental review of social learning

Ishikawa, M., & Itakura, S. (2024). “The development of social learning: from pedagogical cues to selective learning.” *Frontiers in Psychology*, 15, 1466618. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1466618

The review summarizes developmental studies using gaze following, attention to social cues, word-object associations, tool use, action-sequence learning, and imitation as outcomes. It distinguishes imitation from mimicry and notes that ostensive signals may help partly by attracting attention, not only by communicating intention. Thus, a cue effect is an outcome under a particular design and does not automatically identify one unique mental mechanism.

## Source 3 — review of social learning in humans and other animals

Gariépy, J.-F., Watson, K. K., Du, E., Xie, D. L., Amasino, D., & Platt, M. L. (2014). “Social learning in humans and other animals.” *Frontiers in Neuroscience*, 8, 58. https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2014.00058

This review distinguishes emulation from imitation. Emulation involves learning about the goal or outcome and independently discovering actions that reach it, often through trial and error. Imitation involves reproducing the goal and the action sequence used to reach it. The review emphasizes that a particular experimental setup may not cleanly distinguish the two, because an observer can copy an outcome or device interaction without reproducing the model’s actions.

## Evidence-bounded conclusion

Social-learning studies measure behavior after defined social exposure. Depending on the design, researchers may record attention or gaze, later choices, object or word associations, tool use, copied actions, goal attainment, retention, or transfer. These are different outcomes. A successful outcome may reflect emulation or individual problem solving, while sequence copying provides different evidence from outcome matching. The sources do not support calling one copied response proof of a universal learning trait, direct access to mental states, or guaranteed real-world transfer.
