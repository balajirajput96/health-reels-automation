# Reel 0053 retrieval audit: framing effect and what studies measure

## Retrieval scope

The retrieval focused on peer-reviewed research distinguishing risky-choice framing, attribute framing, and goal framing, and on evidence about heterogeneity, moderators, response mode, and generalizability.

## Verified findings

**Kühberger (1998), Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, DOI 10.1006/obhd.1998.2781.** This meta-analysis examined the influence of framing on risky decisions and concluded that the overall framing effect was small to moderate, with substantial differences across research designs. Its central boundary is methodological: effect size depends on how the risky decision and frame are operationalized, so a single undifferentiated “framing effect” is too broad.

**Piñón and Gambara (2005), Psicothema, article 3107.** This meta-analytic review identified 51 independent primary studies, 151 effect-size estimates, and nearly 13,500 participants published between 1997 and 2003. It separated risky, attribute, and goal framing. Reported average standardized effects were approximately d=0.437 for risky framing, d=0.260 for attribute framing, and d=0.444 for goal framing. The review reported significant heterogeneity and analyzed moderators including participant type, design, frame type, number of options, response mode, domain, and problem type. The values are historical synthesis estimates, not a guarantee for a new task.

**DeKay, Rubinchik, Li, and De Boeck (2022), Perspectives on Psychological Science, DOI 10.1177/17456916221079611.** Three metastudies with total N=2,338 tested the risky-choice framing effect across many potential moderators. The effect generalized across most tested moderators, but one manipulation involving replacing a certain option with a slightly risky option produced a framing effect up to twice as large, contrary to some theoretical predictions. This shows why broad generalization claims must be tied to the exact option structure and moderator design.

## Definitions and operational distinctions

A risky-choice frame presents formally equivalent uncertain outcomes in gain versus loss terms and measures a change in risky versus certain choice. Attribute framing changes the positive or negative description of an attribute and often measures evaluation rather than risk choice. Goal framing presents the consequences of taking or not taking an action and often measures persuasion or intention. The same words “gain” and “loss” can therefore refer to different task structures.

## Evidence boundaries

A framing effect is an observed difference between responses to semantically different descriptions of a formally equivalent task. It is not direct proof of irrationality, a fixed personality trait, or a universal prediction about any real-world decision. Studies often use student samples, between-subjects designs, hypothetical scenarios, forced-choice tasks, self-report judgments, or specific domains such as health and economics. Wording, time pressure, response mode, option structure, context, and participants can moderate the result. The reel is public education only and does not give medical, political, financial, or individualized decision advice.
