# R0071 Evidence Record: Belonging — What Studies Measure

## Research question and unique angle

This reel asks what a belonging study actually records. The angle is to distinguish a subjective sense of fit or connection from structural contact, perceived support, loneliness, intervention-related uncertainty, and downstream outcomes such as engagement or academic performance.

## Evidence table

| Measurement class | Example operationalization | Direct outcome | Boundary on interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Subjective belonging | Report feeling accepted, connected, or an integral part of a group or place | Perceived belonging score | A defined experience in a context, not a direct count of relationships |
| Structural connection | Network size, contact frequency, living situation, relationship status | Objective or behavioral social-integration indicator | Contact quantity is not identical to felt belonging or support quality |
| Perceived support and loneliness | Report whether support feels sufficient or whether one feels isolated | Subjective support or loneliness score | Related dimensions, not interchangeable with belonging |
| Intervention process | Belonging uncertainty or interpretation of common adversity | Process measure in a specified intervention | Not a universal mechanism for every person or setting |
| Downstream outcome | Engagement, persistence, well-being, performance, mentorship | Academic, health, or social outcome | An outcome is not itself a direct belonging score |

## Source 1 — conceptual and integrative belonging review

Allen, K.-A., Kern, M. L., Rozek, C. S., McInerney, D., & Slavich, G. M. (2021). “Belonging: A Review of Conceptual Issues, an Integrative Framework, and Directions for Future Research.” *Australian Journal of Psychology*, 73, 87–102. https://doi.org/10.1080/00049530.2021.1883409

The review defines belonging as a subjective feeling of deep connection with social groups, places, and individual or collective experiences. Its integrative framework highlights competencies, opportunities, motivations, and perceptions. The review also emphasizes that belonging is dynamic and exists in connection with social systems and context. Thus, a belonging questionnaire generally measures a perceived experience or construct in a defined setting, not a direct census of someone’s relationships.

## Source 2 — social-connectedness subdomains

Gordon, N. P., & Stiefel, M. C. (2024). “A brief but comprehensive three-item social connectedness screener for use in social risk assessment tools.” *PLOS ONE*, 19, e0307107. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0307107

This paper distinguishes social-contact frequency, perceived social/emotional support, and loneliness or social isolation. It compares subjective reports with structural indicators such as network size, living situation, relationship status, and contact frequency. The authors report that loneliness/social-isolation and support-risk variables related more strongly to the paper’s emotional-health self-report measures than contact frequency alone in their cross-sectional survey. The safe interpretation is dimensional: more contact does not automatically equal more belonging, and perceived support is not the same variable as contact quantity.

## Source 3 — randomized belonging intervention

Brady, S. T., Cohen, G. L., Jarvis, S. N., & Walton, G. M. (2020). “A brief social-belonging intervention in college improves adult outcomes for Black Americans.” *Science Advances*, 6, eaay3689. https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.aay3689

This randomized follow-up examined a one-hour first-year college intervention that framed common social and academic adversity as normal and temporary. Reported adult outcomes included career satisfaction and success, psychological well-being, community involvement and leadership, with college mentorship examined as a statistical mediator. The study focused on Black American college students in a defined educational context. It therefore provides evidence about a specified belonging-related process and specified outcomes under studied conditions, not a universal belonging intervention or a replacement for directly measuring belonging.

## Evidence-bounded conclusion

Belonging studies may measure a felt sense of fit or connection, contact and network structure, perceived support, loneliness, belonging uncertainty, or downstream outcomes. These measures answer different questions. A contact count is not the same as felt belonging. A loneliness score is not a complete belonging score. Academic performance or well-being is an outcome, not direct mind-reading. Even a randomized intervention result is bounded by its population, setting, process, and measured outcomes.
