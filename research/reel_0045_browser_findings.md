# Reel 0045 research retrieval audit

## Research question

Reel 0045 asks: **When compassion practice is studied, what counts as evidence of “more compassion”?** The safe answer is that researchers may use self-report scales, observed helping or donation behavior, economic games, visual attention, fMRI responses, or physiological outcomes. These measures target different constructs and do not automatically establish a stable trait or real-world change.

## Leiberg et al. (2011) checked details

The paper used two experiments. Experiment 1 introduced the Zurich Prosocial Game (ZPG), designed to repeatedly assess helping while varying reciprocity, helping cost, and distress cues. In the task, the authors reported more helping when reciprocity was present, helping cost was low, and, in the first experiment, distress cues were present. The game correlated with a dictator-game measure of giving, while a memory task was used as a divergent-validity comparison.

Experiment 2 compared short-term compassion training with short-term memory training. The authors reported increased helping in the ZPG after compassion training but not after memory training. Interindividual differences in practice duration were related to changes in helping under no-reciprocity conditions. This is a study-specific behavioral result in a computer game, not proof that every compassion practice changes everyday behavior.

The paper distinguished a prosocial game from self-report and from a monetary dictator game. It also tested contextual factors such as reciprocity and helping cost. Reel 0045 uses this to show that the “outcome” can be a choice to help under a designed condition, not an all-purpose compassion score.

## Luberto et al. (2017) checked details

The review included 26 randomized controlled trials with 1,714 participants. The abstract reported small-to-medium pooled effects on self-reported outcomes (SMD = .40) and observable outcomes (SMD = .45). The included studies used a range of practices, populations, controls, and outcome instruments. The review noted that most studies measured outcomes soon after intervention and only four included longer-term follow-up, ranging from 8 to 52 weeks. Outcome categories included validated self-report measures, computerized donation tasks, real-time helping behavior, peer-rated prosociality, and other objective or observable measures.

The methods extracted intervention dose and adherence, prosocial outcomes and time points, and possible mechanisms. Risk-of-bias assessment classified 11 studies as low risk, 12 as medium risk, and three as high risk. Most controls were wait-list or no-treatment, with fewer active controls. Reel 0045 will state that a meta-analysis can summarize average effects across selected trials, but it does not turn heterogeneous short-term measures into a guaranteed personal transformation.

## Weng et al. (2018) checked details

Healthy adults were randomized to two weeks of compassion or reappraisal training. The full protocol included 30 minutes daily for two weeks, with at least 11 of 14 practice days required. In the analyzed subset with usable eye-tracking data at both scans, 24 participants remained, split into 12 compassion and 12 reappraisal participants. During fMRI, participants viewed images depicting human suffering or non-suffering while eye tracking was recorded. Visual preference was defined as looking time in socially and emotionally evocative regions, and the study also analyzed BOLD responses, including amygdala responses.

The abstract reported that increased visual preference for suffering after compassion training was associated with decreased amygdala responses, and described this as a study-specific association. The paper’s methods make clear that the neural and eye-tracking outcomes were recorded during a designed laboratory task. They are not a direct readout of moral character, private thoughts, or general compassion in every setting.

## Evidence boundaries

The reel may say that compassion research can measure self-reported compassion, helping choices in a game, donation or redistribution decisions, visual attention to suffering, fMRI BOLD responses, or physiological markers. It may say that training dose and adherence are exposure variables while the outcome is task- or questionnaire-specific. It may say that randomization supports comparison of assigned training conditions and that meta-analysis estimates average effects across included studies.

The reel must not say that compassion training makes everyone kinder, that a game proves real-world altruism, that the amygdala is a compassion meter, or that an effect persists indefinitely. No diagnosis, treatment, individualized practice instruction, moral judgment, guaranteed benefit, or inference about a person’s character from one score will be given.

## AI and safety disclosure

Hindi narration will be AI-generated. Visuals will be deterministic conceptual motion graphics showing self-report, helping behavior, eye tracking, and fMRI as separate measurement channels. No participant likenesses, clinical scans, or private-thought readouts will be used. No social publication is authorized or attempted.
