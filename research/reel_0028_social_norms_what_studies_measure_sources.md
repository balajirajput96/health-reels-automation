# Reel 0028 — social norms: studies actually measure what?

## Evidence question

This reel asks how social norms are operationalized in research. It distinguishes what people think most others do, what people think others approve or disapprove of, observed behavior, choices in a social task, and model-derived or neural correlates of expectation violations. It does not treat a survey response as a universal moral rule or infer a viewer’s values from one answer.

## Vetted evidence map

| Evidence point | What the source supports | Boundary for narration |
|---|---|---|
| “Social norm” is a family of related constructs | Reviews show the field contains multiple theories and recurring disagreements about what norms are and how they relate to behavior | Do not present one definition as universally settled |
| Descriptive and injunctive expectations differ | Descriptive expectations concern what people are believed to do; injunctive expectations concern what people are believed to approve or disapprove | A perceived norm is not the same thing as actual population behavior or personal preference |
| Norms can be measured with self-report and reference-group questions | Researchers may ask participants about typical behavior, approval, disapproval, sanctions, or relevant reference groups | Self-report is a report of belief or perception, not proof of a group’s actual conduct |
| Social influence can be tested in behavioral tasks | Bargaining, coordination, conformity, choice, and other tasks compare decisions under different social information or expectations | One task is not a global measure of morality, conformity, or character |
| Computational and neural variables are study-specific | Social decision studies can model expected outcomes or violations and relate them to behavior and brain activity | Model parameters and fMRI signals are correlates under assumptions, not direct mind-reading |

## Primary and review sources

1. Legros, S., & Cislaghi, B. (2019). “Mapping the Social-Norms Literature: An Overview of Reviews.” *Perspectives on Psychological Science*, 15(1), 62–80. DOI: [10.1177/1745691619866455](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691619866455). This review maps theoretical spaces around definitions of norms, norm–behavior relationships, norm evolution, and relevant actors or reference groups, emphasizing that the literature is multifaceted and sometimes contradictory.

2. Chang, L. J., & Sanfey, A. G. (2013). “Great expectations: neural computations underlying the use of social norms in decision-making.” *Social Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience*, 8(3), 277–284. DOI: [10.1093/scan/nsr094](https://doi.org/10.1093/scan/nsr094). The study combined a social bargaining task, computational modeling, and fMRI. It reported that a model based on social expectations explained decision behavior and that expectation violations were tracked by anterior cingulate activity in that task.

3. Cialdini, R. B., Reno, R. R., & Kallgren, C. A. (1990). “A focus theory of normative conduct: Recycling the concept of norms to reduce littering in public places.” *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 58(6), 1015–1026. DOI: [10.1037/0022-3514.58.6.1015](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.58.6.1015). This foundational experimental paper is used for the distinction between descriptive information about what is commonly done and injunctive information about what is approved or disapproved, while the reel avoids claiming that every context produces the same effect.

4. Lapinski, M. K., & Rimal, R. N. (2005). “An Explication of Social Norms.” *Communication Theory*, 15(2), 127–147. DOI: [10.1111/j.1468-2885.2005.tb00329.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2885.2005.tb00329.x). This conceptual review is used to frame norms as socially shared expectations connected to behavior and communication, while preserving the distinction between perceived expectations, sanctions, and observed behavior.

## Research and safety limits

Norm measures depend on the reference group, wording, culture, context, timing, incentive structure, and whether the outcome is a belief, a choice, or observed behavior. A perceived norm is not automatically a real group average, and a single choice cannot diagnose personality, morality, or social ability. The reel will avoid coercion tactics, targeted persuasion, political persuasion, stigma, individualized social judgment, and claims that brain activity reveals a viewer’s values. AI narration and deterministic conceptual visuals will be disclosed.
