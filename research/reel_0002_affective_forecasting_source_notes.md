# Reel 0002 source notes — affective forecasting measurement

## Verified primary source

Gilbert, Pinel, Wilson, Blumberg, and Wheatley (1998), “Immune neglect: a source of durability bias in affective forecasting,” *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 75(3), 617–638. PubMed PMID 9781405; DOI 10.1037//0022-3514.75.3.617. PubMed abstract reports six studies across romantic-relationship dissolution, tenure failure, election defeat, negative personality feedback, an account of a child’s death, and rejection by a prospective employer. The studies compared people’s forecasts of how long negative affect would last with their later reported experience and found overestimation of duration in the tested contexts; the paper interprets this as neglect of psychological coping mechanisms. Reel wording must say “in the tested studies/contexts,” not “everyone always predicts wrong.”

## Planned evidence boundary

The reel’s unique angle is measurement: a typical affective-forecasting design asks participants to predict a future emotional response and then compares that forecast with later self-reported experience. The script must distinguish predicted intensity/duration from experienced intensity/duration, and must not imply a diagnosis, treatment, guaranteed decision improvement, or universal forecasting error.

## Cross-check review

Wilson and Gilbert (2005), “Affective Forecasting: Knowing What to Want,” *Review of General Psychology*, PDF: https://dtg.sites.fas.harvard.edu/WILSON%20%26%20GILBERT%20(2005).pdf. The opened PDF defines affective forecasts as predictions about emotional reactions to future events and discusses errors involving the predicted intensity and duration of future affect. It distinguishes the “impact bias” from other forms of misprediction and emphasizes that the review concerns decisions based on forecasts, not a universal claim that people cannot predict feelings. This review is used for terminology and measurement framing; the 1998 PubMed article is the primary empirical anchor.
