# Reel 0047 research retrieval audit

## Research question

Reel 0047 asks: **What does a retrieval-practice study actually measure?** The safe distinction is between assigned or available retrieval opportunities, a learner’s observed participation and answer performance, immediate or delayed retention tests, and transfer to new formats or applications. A better final score on one test is not automatically proof of a general learning habit or universal transfer.

## Azzam & Easteal (2021) checked details

This quasi-experimental study took place in a large undergraduate Gross Anatomy course at Queen’s University. It included 248 students. Retrieval practice was implemented during the first ten minutes of lectures, with six to ten questions per lecture. A total of 252 retrieval-practice questions were posed during the semester. Correct-answer feedback was provided one day after each session, and the final exam was written about three weeks after the course ended.

The study used de-identified participation data from the TopHat platform. Participation was defined as the total number of retrieval-practice questions answered, regardless of correctness. Students were divided at an 85% participation cut-off into higher- and lower-participation groups. The outcome was final-exam performance, with 67 questions considered congruent with retrieval-practice content. The higher-participation group had higher average scores on those final-exam questions, and participation showed moderate positive correlations with final-exam performance.

The paper itself describes a quasi-experimental design, not a randomized experiment. It notes possible confounding: students who participated more might be more conscientious, interested, or academically prepared. Students may also have answered together, so platform participation did not always prove an individual retrieval attempt. The result is therefore an association in one anatomy course and one assessment context, not a universal causal guarantee.

## Pan & Rickard (2018) checked details

The meta-analysis included 192 transfer effect sizes from 122 experiments and 67 published or unpublished articles, totaling 10,382 participants. A random-effects model found a positive average transfer effect relative to a non-testing re-exposure control, d = 0.40 with a 95% confidence interval of [0.31, 0.50]. Transfer was stronger across test formats and for application and inference questions under some moderators, and weaker for certain rearranged stimulus-response items, untested materials, and worked examples.

The abstract emphasizes that response congruency, elaborated retrieval practice, and initial test performance influence the likelihood of positive transfer. Publication-bias assessments reduced some intercept predictions and often indicated no positive transfer when the named moderators were absent. Thus “retrieval practice transfers everywhere” is too strong. Transfer depends on the new task, material, test format, feedback or elaboration, and the learner’s initial performance.

## Evidence boundaries

The reel may say that retrieval practice is an observable attempt to recall, while retention is measured later by a test. It may distinguish participation count, answer correctness, final-exam score, delayed retention, and transfer to new question formats. It may say that a quasi-experimental classroom study found higher final-exam performance among higher-participation students, while acknowledging confounding. It may say that a meta-analysis found average transfer relative to re-exposure, but that moderators and task similarity matter.

The reel must not say that any quiz guarantees learning, that a participation log proves successful retrieval, that one anatomy course proves effects in every subject, or that practice automatically transfers to real-world expertise. Do not give individualized educational prescriptions or diagnose ability, intelligence, attention, or motivation from one score.
