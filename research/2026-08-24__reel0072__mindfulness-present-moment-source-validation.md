# REEL-0072 Source Validation: Mindfulness as Noticing Present-Moment Experience

## Editorial question

How can a short Hindi reel explain mindfulness as a present-moment noticing practice without presenting it as a guaranteed stress cure, treatment, or spiritual doctrine?

## Claim register

| Claim | Evidence class | Support | Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mindfulness can be described as intentionally noticing thoughts, feelings, sensations, and surroundings in the present without judgment. | Institutional explanation/definition | NIH News in Health defines mindfulness as awareness of what is happening inside and around us, observed without judgment [1]. Harvard Health similarly describes purposely focusing attention on the present moment [2]. | This is a description of a practice, not proof of a particular health outcome. |
| Breath, body sensations, walking, eating, and ordinary activities can be used as attention anchors. | Institutional practical guidance | NIH and Harvard Health describe returning attention to breathing or sensations and practicing mindfulness while walking, eating, or doing everyday tasks [1] [2]. | The reel should present these as optional cues, not mandatory instructions or a universal meditation method. |
| Mindfulness may support well-being or some mental-health outcomes for some people. | General institutional summary of research | NIH reports that studies suggest possible benefits for health and well-being and describes evidence for some mindfulness-based treatments [1]. | The source is a public-facing summary, not a guarantee. Do not claim instant calm, universal emotional regulation, cure, or replacement of professional care. |
| Returning attention after the mind wanders is part of the practice. | Practice explanation | Both NIH and Harvard explain noticing when attention has wandered and gently returning it to the present or breath [1] [2]. | Avoid framing wandering thoughts as failure or implying that the practice should eliminate thoughts. |
| Mindfulness takes practice and is not necessarily easy. | Expert/institutional guidance | NIH notes that mindfulness is a skill that takes practice and that simple does not mean easy [1]. | Do not promise a fixed timeline or imply moral failure if the practice feels difficult. |

## Editorial boundaries

The reel will focus narrowly on the definition and a gentle everyday cue: notice one breath or sensory detail, notice when attention wanders, and return without judging. It will not teach a full meditation protocol, diagnose anxiety or depression, promise treatment, or conflate mindfulness with a specific religious or spiritual belief.

A short disclaimer will state that this is general education, not psychological or medical treatment. If a practice feels distressing or a person is dealing with significant symptoms, they should seek appropriate professional support rather than treating a reel as care.
