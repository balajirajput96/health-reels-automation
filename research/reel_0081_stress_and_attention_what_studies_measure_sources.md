# R0081 sources and evidence boundaries

## Research question

When a study says that stress affects attention, what was actually measured? This reel explains the operational distinction between stressor exposure, subjective appraisal, attention-task behavior, physiology or EEG, timing, context, and outcomes.

## Opened sources

1. Liu, Q., Liu, Y., Leng, X., Han, J., Xia, F., & Chen, H. (2020). *Impact of Chronic Stress on Attention Control: Evidence from Behavioral and Event-Related Potential Analyses*. Neuroscience Bulletin, 36(11), 1395–1410. DOI: [10.1007/s12264-020-00549-9](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12264-020-00549-9). PMCID: [PMC7674527](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7674527/). The Attention Network Test separates alerting, orienting, and executive/conflict effects; response time, accuracy, and EEG event-related potentials are distinct readouts.

2. Crosswell, A. D., & Lockwood, K. G. (2020). *Best practices for stress measurement: How to measure psychological stress in health research*. Health Psychology Open. DOI: [10.1177/2055102920933072](https://doi.org/10.1177/2055102920933072). Stressor exposure and stress response are different; self-report, behavior, physiological measures, and neural measures should be selected and timed to the question.

3. Epel, E. S., Crosswell, A. D., Mayer, S. E., Prather, A. A., Slavich, G. M., Puterman, E., & Mendes, W. B. (2018). *More than a feeling: A unified view of stress measurement for population science*. Frontiers in Neuroendocrinology, 49, 146–169. DOI: [10.1016/j.yfrne.2018.03.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.yfrne.2018.03.001). The review organizes stress measurement across acute, event-based, daily, and chronic timescales and cautions that self-report, neural, and physiological measures may be loosely related.

4. Sänger, J., Bechtold, L., Schoofs, D., Blaszkewicz, M., & Wascher, E. (2014). *The influence of acute stress on attention mechanisms and its electrophysiological correlates*. Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience, 8, 353. DOI: [10.3389/fnbeh.2014.00353](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnbeh.2014.00353). In a controlled human experiment, a socially evaluated cold pressor stressor preceded a visual attention task; cortisol and alpha-amylase, error rate, and EEG attention components were measured under specified conditions.

## Evidence map

| Dimension | Operationalized example | Bounded interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Exposure | examination context, cold pressor, or social evaluation | a defined stressor or context was present |
| Subjective response | perceived stress, anxiety, calmness, or appraisal | a participant’s report at a stated time |
| Attention behavior | response time, accuracy, error rate, congruency effects | performance on the named task |
| Physiology/neural | cortisol, alpha-amylase, EEG ERP components | biological or neural correlates under stated conditions |
| Timing | before, during, after; acute, daily, chronic | the window in which the result was observed |
| Context | sample, instructions, control condition, repeated observations | scope and transferability of the comparison |
| Outcome | short-task performance or an association with functioning/health | a measured association or group contrast, not automatic causality |

## Allowed claims

The reel may say that attention is often operationalized with accuracy, response time, conflict or cue conditions, while EEG components can provide time-resolved neural correlates. It may say that studies can measure stress exposure, appraisal, physiology, and attention behavior separately, and that timing and task design affect interpretation. It may describe the specific acute-stress experiment as a bounded human laboratory finding.

## Excluded claims

Do not state that stress always reduces attention, that a cortisol or EEG value directly reveals a person’s mind, that a single task diagnoses a condition, or that a group difference predicts an individual. Do not present association as proof of causality, and do not give diagnosis, treatment, prescription, or individualized advice.
