# Reel 0065 browser-verified findings — Task switching: what studies measure

## Source 1: Schmitz and Krämer (2023), “Task Switching: On the Relation of Cognitive Flexibility with Cognitive Capacity,” *Journal of Intelligence*, DOI 10.3390/jintelligence11040068

URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10140903/

The paper describes task switching as a laboratory paradigm used to study processes involved in switching between task sets. Participants perform task repetitions and task switches, often after a task cue. Researchers compare reaction time and accuracy between conditions and may decompose the task-switch effect into component processes using diffusion modeling or latent difference models. The study reports that switch trials can involve longer non-decision time, interpreted in relation to task-set preparation, while response congruency can have a separate effect on drift rate.

The measurement is therefore a controlled difference between conditions under a particular task design. It can inform preparation, task-set reconfiguration, interference, response selection, and related processes. It does not by itself measure all forms of real-world multitasking or prove a person has a fixed, universal cognitive-flexibility level.

## Source 2: Benini, Koch, and Philipp (2023), “Repetition costs in task switching are not equal to cue switching costs: evidence from a cue-independent context,” *Psychological Research*, DOI 10.1007/s00426-023-01904-x

URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10965575/

The study examines how task repetition versus task switching interacts with response repetition and with task-irrelevant context. It reports that full repetition benefits can arise even without a cue-related context, but the context’s position in the trial matters. Task switching commonly involves maintaining two task sets, and performance differences may reflect reconfiguration, carried-over interference, feature binding, retrieval, priming, or response repetition rather than one isolated ability.

The important operational lesson is that the observed switch or repetition cost depends on the exact trial sequence, cues, responses, contextual features, timing, and scoring. A larger cost is not automatically a pure measure of “poor flexibility,” and a smaller cost is not automatically proof of superior multitasking in daily life.

## Safe operational categories

**Task-switch cost:** the difference in reaction time or accuracy between switch and repeat trials in a specified paradigm.

**Mixing cost:** the contrast between performance in a mixed-task block and a single-task or pure-repetition condition, when the design includes both.

**Cue or preparation effects:** the influence of advance task cues, cue-switches, or preparation intervals on performance.

**Response and context effects:** response repetition, feature binding, priming, context repetition, and interference from prior trials.

**Component-model estimates:** diffusion-model parameters or latent differences intended to separate preparation, evidence accumulation, response caution, or other components. These are model-derived estimates and depend on the model and data.

**Accuracy and reaction time:** observable behavioral outcomes under controlled instructions, not direct readouts of thought content or a complete real-world multitasking score.

## Boundary conclusion

Task-switching experiments measure performance differences produced by controlled transitions between task sets, cues, responses, and contexts. They can inform specific cognitive-control processes and their interactions. The same label “switch cost” may combine several components, so interpretation must name the design and contrast. A single task-switching score should not be used to diagnose a person, rank their worth, or guarantee everyday multitasking ability.

## Source 3: Kiesel et al. (2010), “Control and interference in task switching—A review,” *Psychological Bulletin*, DOI 10.1037/a0019842

URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20804238/

This review describes task switching as a way to investigate cognitive control and task interference. Its key implication for measurement is that the observed switch cost can reflect multiple control demands, including preparing or reconfiguring the currently relevant task set and overcoming interference from the previously active task set. Therefore, switch cost is a behavioral contrast generated by a particular task sequence and timing, not a single pure readout of an abstract ability.

The review supports describing task-switching performance in terms of controlled task transitions, interference, preparation, and response selection. It does not justify treating one laboratory contrast as a complete measure of everyday multitasking, personality, intelligence, or clinical status.
