# Reel 0044 research retrieval audit

## Research question

Reel 0044 asks: **When a study says breath awareness was measured, what was actually observed?** The safe distinction is between breath-counting accuracy, subjective awareness or mind-wandering reports, respiratory physiology, and interoceptive detection tasks. None of these alone is a complete measure of mindfulness, attention, or health.

## Levinson et al. (2014) checked details

The study presented breath counting as a behavioral task in which participants tracked breaths and responded to count probes. A physiologically checked subset of 52 participants showed mean keypress rate tracking mean breath rate at *r* = 0.99. Across the total sample, the average error rate was 22% and a mean of 29% of errors were self-caught. In the reported sample, accuracy correlated with meta-awareness and with less state mind wandering. A separate sample of 93 also included working-memory and trait-mindfulness questionnaires; the authors reported small correlations between accuracy and MAAS or FFMQ scores.

The article also reported a randomized online training study in which four weeks of breath-counting training improved the task and some self-report outcomes. Reel 0044 avoids turning those study-specific associations or training findings into proof of universal mindfulness, general attention, or clinical benefit. Breath counting is presented as a task-specific performance measure that can be affected by counting, timing, probe, and self-report demands.

## Daubenmier et al. (2013) checked details

The study examined respiratory interoceptive accuracy in experienced meditators and nonmeditators using a respiratory detection task with controlled resistances and respiratory recording. The overall mean detection accuracy was 83.2 ± 16.2%. The groups did not differ significantly in mean detection accuracy across all resistors: 84.2 ± 17.9% for meditators and 82.2 ± 14.6% for nonmeditators, *t*(32) = 0.35, *p* = .73. The authors noted a high overall detection rate and possible ceiling effect. For a lower-ceiling resistor, the reported group difference was not significant.

In a separate discrimination analysis, overall accuracy was also high. The authors reported no significant group difference across all resistors, while a lower-ceiling resistor showed a borderline group difference. The study therefore supports the distinction between a respiratory detection task and a broad claim about mindfulness. The task used respiratory physiology, but the measured outcome was task performance, not direct access to subjective awareness or private thoughts.

## Evidence boundaries

The reel may say that breath counting can yield a behavioral accuracy score, that researchers can record respiratory signals during a detection task, and that participants may also answer mind-wandering or mindfulness questionnaires. It may say that these measures target different constructs and that ceiling effects, task demands, sample size, and comparison design matter.

The reel must not say that breath-counting accuracy equals mindfulness, that a breathing sensor reads thoughts, that a meditator has universally better interoception, or that changing breathing rate treats a condition. No breathing exercise prescription, individualized advice, diagnosis, treatment, guarantee, or claim of total attention measurement will be given.

## AI and safety disclosure

Hindi narration will be AI-generated. Visuals will be deterministic conceptual motion graphics showing a breath-count task, respiratory waveform, and measurement distinctions. No participant likenesses, clinical scans, or literal thought readouts will be used. No social publication is authorized or attempted.
