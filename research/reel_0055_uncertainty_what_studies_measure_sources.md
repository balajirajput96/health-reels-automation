# Reel 0055 — Uncertainty: what studies actually measure

## Research question
When a study says it measured “uncertainty,” what observable did it actually measure, and what claims remain outside that measure?

## Verified sources

1. **Franssen, M. M., Verstegen, R., Bazilinskyy, P., van der Spek, E. D., & Martens, M. H. (2026).** Psychological uncertainty in human experience and behavior: a systematic review on measurements, induction, and effects. *Frontiers in Psychology, 17*. DOI: [10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1844980](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1844980). Source ID: `doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1844980`. The review synthesized 27 peer-reviewed articles and reports that uncertainty is used across contexts, is not a single unified construct, and is most frequently measured with Likert or multiple-choice self-report. It also describes recall, information withholding/variation, and altered predictability as induction methods.

2. **Brunyé, T. T., & Giles, G. E. (2023).** Methods for eliciting and measuring behavioral and physiological consequences of stress and uncertainty in virtual reality. *Frontiers in Virtual Reality, 4*. DOI: [10.3389/frvir.2023.951435](https://doi.org/10.3389/frvir.2023.951435). Source ID: `doi:10.3389/frvir.2023.951435`. The methods article manipulates stimulus clarity to induce transient uncertainty in perceptual decisions and separates task outcomes—accuracy, discriminability, decision criterion, response time, and confidence—from physiological and biochemical signals such as heart rate, pupil diameter, salivary alpha-amylase, and cortisol.

3. **Anderson, E. C., Carleton, R. N., Diefenbach, M., & Han, P. K. J. (2019).** The relationship between uncertainty and affect. *Frontiers in Psychology, 10*, 2504. DOI: [10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02504](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02504). Source ID: `doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02504`. This review distinguishes uncertainty from mere ignorance and discusses probability/risk, ambiguity, and complexity as different sources. It reviews theoretical relationships with affect and identifies knowledge gaps; it is not a systematic review of every empirical study.

4. **Windschitl, P. D., & Wells, G. L. (1996).** Measuring psychological uncertainty: verbal versus numeric methods. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied, 2*(4), 343–364. DOI: [10.1037/1076-898X.2.4.343](https://doi.org/10.1037/1076-898X.2.4.343). Source ID: `doi:10.1037/1076-898X.2.4.343`. The paper compares verbal and numeric elicitation formats and argues that alternative verbal methods can have advantages in some contexts. It does not justify saying that verbal scales are universally better.

## Measure map

| What is observed | What it can support | What it cannot directly establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Likert or multiple-choice self-report | Reported experience or judgment in that context | A permanent trait, hidden mental state, or universal reaction |
| Accuracy, d-prime, criterion, response time | Task performance and decision behavior | Subjective uncertainty by itself |
| Confidence rating | Reported confidence and calibration when paired with accuracy | Correctness or ability without the task context |
| Heart rate, pupil diameter, salivary alpha-amylase, cortisol | Physiological or biochemical response associated with the study manipulation | A unique psychological meaning or direct “uncertainty meter” |
| Model-derived probability or computational parameter | A formal model’s estimate under its assumptions | A literal measurement of a person’s mind without validation |

## Evidence and safety boundary
The reel uses established measurement descriptions and careful distinctions among self-report, task behavior, confidence, physiological/biochemical signals, and model-derived quantities. It labels broader links between uncertainty and affect as theory or association, not universal causation. It provides public education only and does not diagnose, treat, prescribe, assess an individual, predict personal outcomes, or offer medical, financial, or other individualized advice.
