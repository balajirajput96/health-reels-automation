# Reel 0048 research retrieval audit

## Research question

Reel 0048 asks: **What does a spacing-effect study actually measure?** The central distinction is between the schedule assigned or chosen, the actual gap between study events, performance at the end of training, and performance after a delay. Immediate learning and delayed retention are not the same outcome, and an “optimal” gap depends on the delay and task.

## Smith & Scarf (2017) checked details

The review defines the spacing effect as the observation that repetitions spaced in time tend to produce stronger memories than repetitions massed closer together. It explicitly distinguishes learning or acquisition—performance at the end of training or on an immediate test—from retention—performance after a delay period. The review emphasizes that these measures represent different aspects of task performance: learning reflects peak performance obtained, whereas retention reflects forgetting over time.

The review surveys long-timescale spacing across skill-related tasks, language-related tasks, and generalization. It notes that spaced practice generally produced better learning and retention than massed practice across many tasks, while also describing age- and task-related differences. The article is a review and proposes a reconsolidation account, so proposed mechanisms must be labeled theory or hypothesis rather than direct proof.

## Cepeda et al. (2008) checked details

The primary study taught more than 1,350 individuals a set of facts. A review gap of up to 3.5 months followed learning, and a final test occurred after a further delay of up to one year. At a given test delay, increasing the interstudy gap first improved and then gradually reduced final-test performance. The optimal gap increased as the final test delay increased.

When the gap was expressed as a proportion of the test delay, the abstract reported an optimal gap of about 20–40% of a one-week test delay and about 5–10% of a one-year test delay. These values describe the study’s fact-learning and delayed-test conditions; they are not a universal personal timetable. The study measured fact recall after specified intervals, not motivation, intelligence, identity, or every form of real-world skill.

## Evidence boundaries

The reel may say that spacing studies compare massed and distributed repetitions or manipulate interstudy gaps, then measure immediate performance or delayed retention. It may say that longer delays can change which gap performs best, using the Cepeda abstract’s reported ranges. It may distinguish assigned schedule from actual adherence and from the final test outcome. It may say that review authors discuss task and age differences and proposed mechanisms.

The reel must not promise that one fixed schedule is optimal for everyone, claim that spacing alone guarantees transfer to complex skills, or confuse immediate fluency with durable retention. It must not treat a study schedule as individualized educational advice or infer intelligence from recall scores.
