# Reel 0053 evidence record: फ्रेमिंग इफ़ेक्ट — studies क्या मापते हैं

## Claim boundary

This reel explains that a framing effect is an observed response difference after equivalent information is worded or structured differently. It separates risky-choice framing, attribute framing, and goal framing, and does not label a person irrational or prescribe a decision.

## Source 1 — risky-choice framing meta-analysis

Kühberger, A. (1998). *The influence of framing on risky decisions: A meta-analysis*. Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 75(1), 23–55. DOI: [10.1006/obhd.1998.2781](https://doi.org/10.1006/obhd.1998.2781). This meta-analysis examined framing effects in risky decisions and reported an overall small-to-moderate effect with substantial differences across research designs. The finding supports a conditional interpretation: operational details of the risky decision and the frame can change the observed effect.

## Source 2 — taxonomy and meta-analytic review

Piñón, A. and Gambara, H. (2005). *A meta-analytic review of framing effect: Risky, attribute and goal framing*. Psicothema, 17(2), 325–331. Article: [3107](https://www.psicothema.com/pii?pii=3107). The review identified 51 independent primary studies, 151 effect-size estimates, and 13,343 participants published between 1997 and 2003. It distinguished risky, attribute, and goal framing. Reported average standardized effects were d=0.437 for risky framing, d=0.260 for attribute framing, and d=0.444 for goal framing, with significant heterogeneity and moderator analyses involving participant type, design, frame type, options, response mode, domain, and problem type. These historical pooled estimates do not guarantee an effect in a new task.

## Source 3 — metastudies and generalizability

DeKay, M. L., Rubinchik, N., Li, Z., and De Boeck, P. (2022). *Accelerating Psychological Science With Metastudies: A Demonstration Using the Risky-Choice Framing Effect*. Perspectives on Psychological Science, 17(6), 1704–1736. DOI: [10.1177/17456916221079611](https://doi.org/10.1177/17456916221079611). Three metastudies with total N=2,338 tested the risky-choice framing effect across potential moderators. The effect generalized across most tested moderators, but one change involving a certain option versus a slightly risky option produced an effect up to twice as large and differed from some theoretical predictions. Exact option structure therefore matters when discussing generalizability.

## Source 4 — recent systematic review

Kühberger, A. (2023). *A systematic review of risky-choice framing effects*. EXCLI Journal, 22, 1012–1031. DOI: [10.17179/excli2023-6169](https://doi.org/10.17179/excli2023-6169). This recent review synthesizes the risky-choice framing literature and is used here to emphasize that the classic gain-versus-loss pattern has been examined across many designs rather than treated as a direct diagnosis of a chooser.

## Operational measure map

| Frame type or observable | What it can describe | What it cannot establish alone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Risky-choice framing | Change in risky versus certain choices after gain/loss wording | Irrationality or a fixed bias in every setting |
| Attribute framing | Change in evaluation of the same attribute described positively or negatively | Risk preference, because risk may not be manipulated |
| Goal framing | Change in persuasion, intention, or response after emphasizing consequences | Actual long-term behavior |
| Response choice | A selected option under the task instructions | The reasoning behind the selection |
| Judgment rating | Perceived attractiveness, acceptability, or confidence | Objective value or best choice |
| Response time | Time taken to answer | Decision quality by itself |
| Moderator condition | How wording, options, context, time pressure, or participant sample alter the effect | A universal person-level rule |

## Safety and scientific limits

The same substantive outcomes can produce different responses when framed differently, but the effect depends on frame type, option structure, context, design, and the measured outcome. A framing effect is not direct mind-reading and does not prove that an individual is irrational. The reel is public education only; it does not provide medical, political, financial, or individualized advice.
