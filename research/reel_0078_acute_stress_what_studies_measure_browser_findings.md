# R0078 browser research findings — initial sources

## 1. Weber, Angerer & Apolinário-Hagen (2022), PLOS One
**Title:** Physiological reactions to acute stressors and subjective stress during daily life: A systematic review on ecological momentary assessment (EMA) studies.
**URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9328558/
**Identifier:** DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0271996; PMID 35895674; PROSPERO CRD42020163178.

The review defines acute stressors as short-term events and describes acute subjective and physiological responses as immediate reactions to an appraisal that available resources or capabilities may be insufficient for demands. It distinguishes actual, perceived, or anticipated threat from measurable responses. The review discusses HPA-axis, autonomic, and immune activation and notes that laboratory psychosocial stressors involving social-evaluative threat, novelty, uncontrollability, or ambivalence can produce stronger immediate HPA-axis responses than some passive physical stressors. It also emphasizes that cortisol responses can be difficult to detect in daily life. These findings support the boundary that a single subjective rating or biomarker is not the whole acute-stress response, and timing, context, and measurement setting matter.

## 2. Crosswell & Lockwood (2020), Health Psychology Open
**Title:** Best practices for stress measurement: How to measure psychological stress in health research.
**URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7359652/
**Identifier:** DOI 10.1177/2055102920933072; PMID 32704379.

The review defines “stress” as an umbrella term and separates stressful-event exposure from stress responses. Stressors are discrete events that may disrupt typical psychological functioning; responses include cognitive, emotional, behavioral, and biological reactions. It states that responses can be measured with self-report, behavioral coding, or physiological measurements. It recommends selecting measures that match the research question and sample, documenting timescale and assessment window, and describing exposure characteristics such as duration, severity, controllability, and domain. The review notes that naturalistic experiences do not always fit neatly into chronic, life-event, daily, or acute categories. These points support the reel’s distinction among event, appraisal, behavior, physiology, and outcome rather than treating “stress” as one interchangeable number.

**Interim synthesis:** Acute-stress research commonly separates short-term stressor exposure from subjective/cognitive response and biological response. Associations across layers depend on timing, task, context, sample, and instrument; they do not by themselves establish diagnosis or a universal causal mechanism.

## 3. Shilton, Laycock & Crewther (2017), Frontiers in Psychology
**Title:** The Maastricht Acute Stress Test (MAST): Physiological and Subjective Responses in Anticipation, and Post-stress.
**URL:** https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00567/full
**Identifier:** DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00567.

This original study evaluates MAST, a laboratory procedure combining elements of the Trier Social Stress Test and Cold Pressor Test. The task was designed to activate the stress system and quantified systolic and diastolic blood pressure, pulse rate, and state-anxiety ratings. In healthy adults, systolic blood pressure remained elevated from baseline for up to 30 minutes after the task, diastolic pressure returned to baseline within 10 minutes, pulse-rate variability was limited, and self-reported state anxiety increased strongly immediately after the procedure. The authors also describe anticipatory changes after instructions were presented and note variability across stress protocols and between laboratory and real-life contexts. The study supports the claim that a laboratory task is an operational manipulation with selected endpoints, not a universal model of all acute stress or overwhelming trauma.

## 4. Campbell & Ehlert (2012), Psychoneuroendocrinology review
**Title:** Acute psychosocial stress: does the emotional stress response correspond with physiological responses?
**URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22260938/
**Identifier:** DOI 10.1016/j.psyneuen.2011.12.010; PMID 22260938.

The review searched TSST studies through August 2011 and included 49 studies that assessed subjective emotional stress and reported associations with acute physiological measures. It reports that significant correlations between cortisol responses and perceived emotional-stress variables were found in approximately 25% of the included studies. The authors describe possible sources of apparent dissociation, including differing assessment approaches, protocol methodology, mediating factors, and interindividual differences. This is a review-level reason not to use a subjective rating as a direct proxy for cortisol or vice versa. The percentage is bounded to the included studies and their criteria; it is not a universal population statistic or a diagnostic threshold.

**Evidence-bounded synthesis:** Acute stress research may operationalize a short-term event or laboratory task, a subjective appraisal/emotional rating, a behavioral response, endocrine or autonomic physiology, and downstream outcomes. These measures can change on different timelines and may show only partial correspondence. Any interpretation must name the instrument, endpoint, time window, task/context, sample, and study design; no single measure reads a person’s complete stress state.
