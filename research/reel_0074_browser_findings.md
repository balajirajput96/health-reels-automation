# R0074 Browser Research Findings

## Source 1 — Wang & Xue (2022), Frontiers in Psychology

URL: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.992372/full

The review describes expectancy beliefs as predictions or beliefs about how well a person can deal with a prospective task, often assessed with questionnaires about perceived ability or self-efficacy. It describes subjective task value as personal perceptions about an activity, including intrinsic/interest value, utility value, attainment value, and cost. The review connects these constructs to task choice, attempts, persistence, engagement, participation, and achievement in educational contexts. It also distinguishes broad expectancy measures from more specific self-efficacy measures. Safe interpretation: these are self-report belief/value constructs linked to behavior and performance in specified educational settings, not direct readings of a person’s overall motivation or guaranteed predictors of individual outcomes.

## Source 2 — Shang, Moss, & Chen (2022), meta-analysis in Journal of Sport and Health Science

URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9923428/

The meta-analysis explains two related dimensions: expectancy beliefs for success and subjective task values. It defines expectancy as how well students think they can meet upcoming success expectations. It describes attainment value as perceived personal importance, intrinsic value as anticipated enjoyment or interest, utility value as perceived future usefulness, and cost as what must be given up, including effort, opportunity, and emotional costs. Safe interpretation: expectancy, value, and cost are distinct constructs that studies operationalize through task-specific beliefs and reported reasons; the framework is not a single universal motivation score.

## Current evidence boundary

The evidence supports a careful reel angle: expectancy–value studies ask questions such as “Can I succeed?” and “Is this task important, enjoyable, useful, or costly?” and then examine relationships with choice, persistence, engagement, or performance. These relationships are context-specific and often rely on self-report plus observed outcomes. They should not be presented as diagnosis, certain prediction, or proof that a person will act in one inevitable way.

## Source 3 — Wigfield & Eccles (2000), Contemporary Educational Psychology

URL: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0361476X99910159

The abstract says the article defines ability beliefs, expectancies for success, and components of subjective task values, compares them with self-efficacy, intrinsic/extrinsic motivation, and interest, and reviews relations of children’s and adolescents’ ability-expectancy beliefs and subjective task values to performance and activity choice. Safe interpretation: the model is a framework for studying specific beliefs and values and their relations to choice/performance; it is not a direct measure of a single inner motivation quantity.

## Evidence-bounded conclusion

R0074 should show that expectancy–value studies measure task-specific beliefs (“Can I succeed?”), subjective values (“Is it important, enjoyable, useful?”), and costs, then relate those measures to choices, persistence, engagement, or performance in a defined context. Self-report constructs and observed outcomes should remain distinct; associations should not be narrated as guaranteed predictions or diagnosis.
