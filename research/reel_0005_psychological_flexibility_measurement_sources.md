# Reel 0005 — Psychological Flexibility: What Studies Measure

## Unique angle
This reel explains that psychological flexibility is a theoretical construct assessed through particular questionnaires and related measures; a score is not a complete personality label, diagnosis, or guarantee of behaviour in every situation.

## Verified sources

### 1. Hsu, Hoffman, and Thomas (2023), *Psychological Assessment*
Title: “Confirmatory Measurement Modeling and Longitudinal Invariance of the CompACT-15: A Short-Form Assessment of Psychological Flexibility.”
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1037/pas0001214
Open record: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10101904/
PMID: 36656724; PMCID: PMC10101904.

The study used confirmatory factor analysis and item-factor analysis in a longitudinal online sample of 523 U.S. adults. It reported support for reducing the 23-item CompACT to a 15-item short form, with a more stable factor structure, acceptable reliability across three latent factors, longitudinal measurement equivalence, and construct-validity relations with theoretically relevant measures. The authors define psychological flexibility as abilities involving present-moment focus and behaviour in pursuit of values while experiencing difficult thoughts and emotions. The paper also states that psychological flexibility remains conceptually related to executive function, self-control, self-regulation, emotion regulation, and personality constructs, and that concerns about discriminant validity remain.

Evidence boundary: this supports a particular instrument’s psychometric performance in its sample; it does not show that one questionnaire fully captures flexibility, nor that a score fixes a person’s identity or predicts every future action.

### 2. Levin et al. (2014), *Journal of Contextual Behavioral Science*
Title: “The Acceptance and Action Questionnaire–Stigma (AAQ-S): Developing a measure of psychological flexibility with stigmatizing thoughts.”
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcbs.2013.11.003
Open record: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4255270/
PMID: 25485230; PMCID: PMC4255270.

In an online survey of 604 undergraduate students, the researchers developed and tested a domain-specific questionnaire for flexibility and inflexibility relating to stigmatizing thoughts. Expert ratings and factor analysis identified two subscales. The subscales and total score correlated with related flexibility and stigma measures in expected directions and were more predictive of stigma than a general flexibility measure in that study.

Evidence boundary: the findings illustrate that context-specific measures can be more sensitive for a specific domain; they do not establish a universal score for psychological flexibility or prove causation.

### 3. Ong, Barthel, and Hofmann (2024), *Behavior Therapy* meta-analysis
Title: “The Relationship Between Psychological Inflexibility and Well-Being in Adults: A Meta-Analysis of the Acceptance and Action Questionnaire.”
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.beth.2023.05.007
Measure overview: https://contextualscience.org/book/export/html/33928

The reported meta-analysis synthesized 151 studies, 25 versions of the Acceptance and Action Questionnaire, and 43 well-being measures. It reported an overall association between higher questionnaire-measured psychological inflexibility and worse well-being, with a pooled correlation of approximately r = −.47 and a 95% confidence interval of −.49 to −.45. The source summary states that sample diagnosis, questionnaire type, and well-being measure moderated the association, and that the reliance on cross-sectional data prevents causal interpretation.

Evidence boundary: this is an association across studies, not proof that inflexibility causes lower well-being, and not a treatment or outcome promise.
