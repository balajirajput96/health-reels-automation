# Reel 0064 browser-verified findings — Mind wandering: what studies measure

## Source 1: Zanesco, Van Dam, Denkova, and Jha (2024), “Measuring mind wandering with experience sampling during task performance: An item response theory investigation,” *Behavior Research Methods*, DOI 10.3758/s13428-024-02446-9

URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11362314/

The article states that mind-wandering studies most commonly use experience-sampling probes asking participants whether attention was on-task or off-task toward task-unrelated thought. Probes may be dichotomous, categorical, or continuous, and can include reports of task focus, thoughts about performance, or kinds of task-unrelated thought. Researchers may summarize responses as a mean rating or proportion of off-task probes.

The authors treat a tendency to mind-wander as a quantitative continuum but emphasize an open measurement question: different probe formats may affect measurement precision. Dichotomizing richer response options can lose information and compromise reliability, statistical power, correlations, and precision of the underlying construct. A probe response is therefore a report about the participant’s ongoing experience at a sampled moment, not an infallible camera on hidden thought.

## Source 2: Nazari, Fitzgerald, and Kazemi (2025), “The Relative Accuracy of Different Methods for Measuring Mind Wandering Subtypes: A Systematic Review,” *Brain and Behavior*, DOI 10.1002/brb3.70764

URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12373717/

This systematic review synthesizes 555 studies and compares several methods. Questionnaires and experience sampling are frequently used and can have ecological value, but they are limited by self-report bias. Neuroimaging, including fMRI and EEG, can provide insights into neural correlates but is costly and often has lower ecological validity. Behavioral tasks can reveal task-related lapses but may be less sensitive to spontaneous mind wandering. Physiological measures and eye tracking add perspectives but require complex analysis.

The review’s central conclusion is that no single method captures all aspects of mind wandering. It proposes integrating methods rather than treating one score or one signal as a complete measurement.

## Safe operational categories

**Self-report and experience sampling:** what participants report about their current thought focus at probe times, with response format and wording influencing the measurement.

**Task performance:** reaction time, accuracy, omissions, variability, or lapses during a task. These can indicate task-related performance changes but do not uniquely identify the content or cause of a thought.

**Questionnaires:** retrospective or trait-like reports about typical mind-wandering tendencies, subject to recall and self-report limitations.

**Neural and physiological correlates:** EEG, fMRI, eye movements, or other signals associated with sampled states. These are proxies linked to the study context, not direct thought-reading instruments.

**Ecological validity and integration:** whether a method generalizes outside the laboratory and whether combining complementary methods provides a more complete picture.

## Boundary conclusion

Mind wandering is a dynamic shift of attention from a primary task toward unrelated thought. Studies may measure a sampled self-report, a task-performance change, a retrospective tendency, or an associated neural/physiological signal. These are different observables. None alone proves exactly what a person is thinking, gives a diagnosis, or guarantees a stable everyday trait.

## Source 3: Christoff et al. (2009), “Experience sampling during fMRI reveals default network and executive system contributions to mind wandering,” *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, DOI 10.1073/pnas.0900234106

URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2689035/

This study combined intermittent experience-sampling probes with fMRI while participants performed a sustained-attention-to-response task. Participants reported their current mental state at probe times, and analyses examined neural activity immediately before reports of on-task versus off-task thought. Performance errors on the concurrent task were used as an independent behavioral index related to mind wandering. The study reported associations between self-reported mind wandering, task errors, and activity in default-network and executive-system regions.

The methodological lesson is that subjective reports, task behavior, and fMRI signals are complementary observables. The neural findings are correlational and tied to a particular task, probe schedule, and analysis window; they do not directly decode a person’s private thoughts. Even experience sampling is a report collected at sampled moments and can differ from continuous or retrospective measurement.
