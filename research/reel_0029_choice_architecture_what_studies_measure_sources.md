# Reel 0029 — choice architecture: studies actually measure what?

## Evidence question

This reel explains how choice architecture is studied without treating a “nudge” as a magic button. It distinguishes an experimentally changed decision environment from the choices people make, self-reported preferences, downstream behavior, and the mechanisms a model proposes.

## Vetted evidence map

| Evidence point | What the source supports | Boundary for narration |
|---|---|---|
| Choice architecture changes presentation or structure | Reviews describe changing how options are organized, described, sequenced, or preselected while leaving options available | Do not call every persuasion or mandate a nudge |
| Common manipulations include defaults and framing | Experiments can compare opt-in versus opt-out defaults, choosing versus rejecting frames, visible information, or alternative structures | A manipulation affects a measured decision in a specified setting; it does not prove a stable preference change |
| Outcomes are usually choices or behavior | Studies record selections, adoption, agreement, purchase, food choice, or other target behaviors; surveys may separately record intentions or preferences | Intention, choice, and long-term real-world behavior are different outcomes |
| Technique and domain matter | Taxonomy and meta-analysis work separates decision information, decision structure, and decision assistance, and reports variation by technique and domain | Average effects do not guarantee an effect for every person or context |
| Publication bias and implementation context matter | Meta-analytic work reports heterogeneity and some publication bias, and primary studies can find framing and default effects that differ by domain | Avoid universal promises or causal claims beyond the design |

## Primary and review sources

1. Münscher, R., Vetter, M., & Scheuerle, T. (2016). “A Review and Taxonomy of Choice Architecture Techniques.” *Journal of Behavioral Decision Making*, 29(5), 511–524. DOI: [10.1002/bdm.1897](https://doi.org/10.1002/bdm.1897). The review proposes three broad categories—decision information, decision structure, and decision assistance—and emphasizes distinguishing intervention techniques from underlying cognitive processes. It also describes defaults, information visibility, social reference points, option effort, and reminders as different intervention forms.

2. Mertens, S., Herberz, M., Hahnel, U. J. J., & Brosch, T. (2022). “The effectiveness of nudging: A meta-analysis of choice architecture interventions across behavioral domains.” *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 119(1), e2107346118. DOI: [10.1073/pnas.2107346118](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2107346118). This meta-analysis pooled more than 200 studies and more than 440 effect sizes. It reports average behavior-change effects that vary by technique and domain, along with moderate publication bias toward positive results. These are aggregate results, not individual guarantees.

3. Chandrashekar, S. P., & Fillon, A. A. (2024). “Framing the Default: Influence of Choosing Versus Rejecting Frame on Default Effects.” *Experimental Psychology*, 71(3), 164–175. DOI: [10.1027/1618-3169/a000617](https://doi.org/10.1027/1618-3169/a000617). This primary study examined 1,072 participants in hypothetical work and health scenarios. It found framing effects in both domains, while default effects were limited in the work domain and absent in the health domain in that study. The result illustrates why effects must be tied to the specific manipulation and context.

4. Johnson, E. J., & Goldstein, D. (2003). “Do Defaults Save Lives?” *Science*, 302(5649), 1338–1339. DOI: [10.1126/science.1091721](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1091721). The university-hosted research record confirms that the article investigated default effects on organ-donation agreement rates in three studies. The reel uses it as a foundational example of comparing choices under different default settings; it does not present the study as proof of a universal or guaranteed real-world effect.

## Research and safety limits

Choice-architecture findings depend on the target behavior, options, wording, default, time horizon, incentives, population, setting, and implementation. A measured choice is not automatically a stable preference, an intention is not long-term behavior, and an average meta-analytic effect is not an individual prediction. The reel will avoid targeted persuasion, deceptive interface tactics, political influence, medical or financial prescriptions, and claims that a design can control a person. AI narration and deterministic conceptual visuals will be disclosed.
