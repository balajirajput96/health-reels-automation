# Reel 0032 — affect labeling and context: studies actually measure what?

## Evidence question

This reel explains how affect-labeling studies operationalize “putting feelings into words.” A label choice, the person’s self-reported experience, a physiological or neural signal, and later behavior are different measurements. Their relationship depends on the task, stimulus, timing, intensity, sample, and comparison condition.

## Vetted evidence map

| Measurement layer | What is recorded | What it does not establish by itself |
|---|---|---|
| Label selection | Which emotion word a participant chooses for a stimulus or reported feeling, sometimes with label-accuracy ratings | It is not a complete readout of the person’s inner emotional state |
| Subjective experience | Ratings such as distress, pleasure, unpleasantness, or confidence after a trial | A rating is self-report in that task and time window, not a permanent trait |
| Task performance | Button press, choice accuracy, reaction time, or performance in a comparison condition | Performance can be influenced by task demands and does not prove a broad emotion skill |
| Physiology or fMRI | Changes in bodily or brain signals during the task | A signal is a correlate; it does not decode exact thoughts or prove a single mechanism |
| Context | Timing, emotional intensity, stimulus type, control condition, population, and sample size | Results from one paradigm should not be generalized into universal advice |

## Vetted scholarly sources

1. Lieberman, M. D., Eisenberger, N. I., Crockett, M. J., Tom, S. M., Pfeifer, J. H., & Way, B. M. (2007). “Putting feelings into words: affect labeling disrupts amygdala activity in response to affective stimuli.” *Psychological Science*, 18(5), 421–428. DOI: [10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01916.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01916.x). PubMed records the affect-labeling fMRI design and reports differences in amygdala, limbic, and right ventrolateral prefrontal activity. The reel describes these as neural correlates of a task, not mind-reading.

2. Lieberman, M. D., Inagaki, T. K., Tabibnia, G., & Crockett, M. J. (2011). “Subjective Responses to Emotional Stimuli During Labeling, Reappraisal, and Distraction.” *Emotion*, 11(3), 468–480. DOI: [10.1037/a0023503](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0023503). The experiments compared affect labeling with watching, reappraisal, and distraction and recorded self-reported emotional experience and participants’ predictions. The reel uses this to distinguish experienced ratings from predictions and avoids presenting the result as guaranteed coping advice.

3. Burklund, L. J., Creswell, J. D., Irwin, M. R., & Lieberman, M. D. (2014). “The common and distinct neural bases of affect labeling and reappraisal in healthy adults.” *Frontiers in Psychology*, 5, 221. DOI: [10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00221](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00221). The study compared affect labeling, reappraisal, observation, and a shape-matching control while combining self-report with fMRI task contrasts. The reel uses it to separate instructions, subjective ratings, and neural measurements.

4. Levy-Gigi, E., & Shamay-Tsoory, S. (2022). “Affect labeling: The role of timing and intensity.” *PLOS ONE*, 17(12), e0279303. DOI: [10.1371/journal.pone.0279303](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0279303). The study used performance-based label choices and distress and label-accuracy ratings while manipulating timing and emotional intensity. Its context-dependent findings support a cautious explanation rather than universal instructions.

## Research and safety limits

Affect-labeling experiments usually compare conditions within a defined paradigm. They do not establish a universal effect for every person, emotion, or situation. Neural activity is not a direct transcript of private thought; self-report is not identical to physiology; and a label choice is not a diagnosis. The reel will not prescribe coping exercises, claim to treat anxiety or depression, or judge viewers’ emotional abilities. It will disclose AI narration and deterministic conceptual visuals.
