# R0073 Browser Research Findings

## Source 1 — Kivetz, Urminsky, and Zheng (2006)

URL: https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/1200/goalgradient.pdf

Primary paper: “The Goal-Gradient Hypothesis Resurrected: Purchase Acceleration, Illusionary Goal Progress, and Customer Retention,” *Journal of Marketing Research*, 43(1), 39–58.

The paper defines the goal-gradient hypothesis as the classic observation that animals expend more effort as they approach a reward, then tests related human consumer behavior. Its abstract describes field experiments, secondary customer data, paper-and-pencil problems, and laboratory experiments. The human operationalizations include coffee-card purchase acceleration as customers approached a free coffee, how quickly people completed online surveys as they neared a monetary incentive, and whether an apparently pre-stamped loyalty card changed progress and completion. Safe interpretation: the observed outcomes are purchase/completion behavior under specified reward programs and protocols, not a universal direct measure of motivation.

## Source 2 — Schmid (2020)

URL: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022103119306705

Primary paper: Petra C. Schmid, “Power reduces the goal gradient effect,” *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*, 90, 104003. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2020.104003.

The abstract reports that low-power participants were less motivated when goal attainment seemed far away than when it seemed close, consistent with the goal-gradient hypothesis. High-power participants’ motivation was less affected by goal proximity. The study also reports that high-power participants set lower goals, were more likely to attain them, and performed better when the goal was far rather than near, whereas low-power participants showed the opposite performance trend. The authors present power as a boundary condition: the goal-gradient effect can reduce or disappear under high experienced power. Safe interpretation: goal proximity was manipulated and motivation/performance were measured in a particular experimental context; this does not justify a universal law for every person, goal, or deadline.

## Current evidence boundary

The two sources support a careful reel angle: researchers measure protocol-specific behavior or self-reported motivation as goal proximity changes, and the pattern can depend on contextual moderators such as experienced power. Do not claim that everyone automatically speeds up near every goal, that a progress bar creates motivation in all settings, or that observed completion/purchase behavior reveals a private motive directly.

## Source 3 — SAGE/Journal of Marketing Research abstract

URL: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1509/jmkr.43.1.39

The abstract reports that participants in a real café reward program purchased coffee more frequently as they approached a free coffee; internet users rating songs for reward certificates visited more often, rated more songs per visit, and persisted longer as they approached the reward; an apparently pre-stamped loyalty card induced faster completion of required purchases; and stronger acceleration toward the goal predicted retention and faster re-engagement in that program. The paper used field experiments, secondary customer data, paper-and-pencil problems, and Tobit/logit models. Safe interpretation: these are purchase, visit, rating, persistence, completion, retention, and re-engagement outcomes under specified incentive systems—not a direct readout of a private motivational state and not evidence that every goal produces the same pattern.

## Evidence-bounded conclusion

Across the accessible sources, goal-gradient research measures observable effort, persistence, purchases, visits, task completion, or self-reported motivation while goal proximity is varied or observed. The pattern is context-sensitive: experienced power can reduce or eliminate the effect, and reward-program design can create apparent progress. The reel should present “what studies measure” rather than promise a universal law that people always accelerate near a finish line.

## Source 4 — Van den Bergh et al. (2016)

URL: https://pure.eur.nl/en/publications/altering-speed-of-locomotion/

The Erasmus University Rotterdam record identifies the article as a peer-reviewed *Journal of Consumer Research* paper. Its abstract reports lab and field experiments in which the number, nature, and salience of progress markers along a path communicated goal progress. Consistent with a goal-gradient account, customers walked faster when fewer progress markers were placed along the path to the goal. The number-of-markers effect diminished when markers were unrelated to the goal and reversed when markers were more salient than the goal. Safe interpretation: the measured outcome was walking speed in a retail environment, and marker meaning/salience moderated the pattern; this is not evidence of a universal internal motivation state.
