# REEL-0076 Source Validation: Notification Settings and Attention Management

## Editorial question

How can a reel explain why smartphone notifications may interrupt attention while avoiding the stronger, unsupported claim that turning off alerts automatically improves focus, productivity, or mental health?

## Claim register

| Claim | Evidence class | Support | Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Smartphone-notification sounds can act as salient cues during a controlled attention task. | Peer-reviewed laboratory study | Upshaw et al. compared smartphone-notification and control sounds in a cognitive-control task and found slower responses on notification-sound trials [1]. | The task was laboratory-based and does not prove that every everyday notification causes the same effect. |
| The notification study also measured ERP differences and individual variation in smartphone-use proneness. | Peer-reviewed laboratory study | Upshaw et al. reported ERP differences and a lower P2 ERP among participants with higher smartphone-addiction proneness on smartphone-sound trials [1]. | Do not translate ERP findings into a clinical assessment, brain damage, or a universal attention deficit. |
| A one-week notification-disabling intervention did not reduce screen time, checking frequency, perceived distraction, productivity, or perceived control in a preregistered RCT. | Randomized controlled trial | Dekker et al. studied 205 participants with objective logs and daily diaries and reported no effects on those outcomes [2]. | This directly limits any promise that disabling alerts will automatically improve focus or digital well-being. |
| Disabling notifications reduced perceived checking-habit strength but increased fear of missing out in that trial. | Randomized controlled trial | Dekker et al. reported a more intentional checking experience alongside increased fear of missing out [2]. | Effects can be mixed and person-dependent; do not recommend disabling every alert. |
| Reviewing non-essential alerts or using a focus mode can be a reversible personal experiment. | Practical synthesis | The findings support testing a user-controlled change while acknowledging mixed evidence [1] [2]. | Preserve safety-critical, work-critical, caregiving, accessibility, and personally important alerts. No fixed setting is universally correct. |

## Editorial boundaries

The reel will say that notification cues can compete for attention in a controlled task, while real-world intervention evidence is mixed. It will recommend a reversible review of non-essential alerts, batching, or a focus mode for one context—not a blanket digital detox.

The reel will not claim that notifications steal a fixed number of minutes, that silence assurances deep work, or that notification settings address anxiety, addiction, ADHD, or any other condition. The advice is general digital-habit education.
