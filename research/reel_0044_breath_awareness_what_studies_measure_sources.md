# Reel 0044 — श्वास-जागरूकता: studies वास्तव में क्या मापते हैं?

## Evidence-bounded claim

Breath-awareness research can measure breath-counting accuracy, task-based respiratory detection, respiratory signals, and subjective reports of awareness or mind-wandering. These are different outcomes. A breath-counting score is not identical to mindfulness, and a respiratory waveform is not a recording of private thoughts.

## Source 1 — Breath counting as a behavioral measure

**Citation:** Levinson, D. B., Stoll, E. L., Kindy, S. D., Merry, H. L., & Davidson, R. J. (2014). *A mind you can count on: validating breath counting as a behavioral measure of mindfulness*. Frontiers in Psychology, 5, 1202. DOI: [10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01202](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01202).

**Evidence class:** Peer-reviewed behavioral validation research with a randomized online-training component.

**Task and measures:** Participants tracked their breaths and responded to count probes. In a physiologically checked subset of 52, mean keypress rate tracked mean breath rate at *r* = 0.99. Across the total sample, the article reported an average error rate of 22% and a mean of 29% of errors being self-caught. Breath-counting accuracy was related to state meta-awareness and less state mind wandering in the reported samples. Separate participants also completed working-memory and trait-mindfulness questionnaires.

**Reported interpretation:** The authors treated breath counting as a behavioral index with relationships to meta-awareness and mind wandering. In a randomized online training study, four weeks of breath-counting training improved breath-counting performance and some self-report outcomes.

**Measurement boundary:** This is performance on a structured counting-and-probe task. It can be influenced by counting skill, timing, probe demands, and the participant’s reporting. It is not a complete meter of mindfulness, attention across daily life, or clinical health.

## Source 2 — Respiratory interoceptive accuracy

**Citation:** Daubenmier, J., Sze, J., Kerr, C. E., Kemeny, M., & Mehling, W. (2013). *Follow your breath: Respiratory interoceptive accuracy in experienced meditators*. Psychophysiology, 50(8), 777–789. DOI: [10.1111/psyp.12057](https://doi.org/10.1111/psyp.12057).

**Evidence class:** Peer-reviewed psychophysiology study.

**Task and measures:** Experienced meditators and nonmeditators performed a respiratory detection task involving controlled resistances while respiratory activity was recorded. The measured outcome was task accuracy in detecting or discriminating respiratory changes, not a direct measure of all subjective awareness.

**Reported findings:** Overall mean detection accuracy across resistors was 83.2 ± 16.2%. The groups did not differ significantly in mean detection accuracy across all resistors: 84.2 ± 17.9% for meditators versus 82.2 ± 14.6% for nonmeditators, *t*(32) = 0.35, *p* = .73. The paper notes high overall accuracy and a possible ceiling effect. A lower-ceiling discrimination condition showed a more limited, borderline group difference.

**Measurement boundary:** A respiratory signal was recorded, but the outcome was accuracy in a particular detection task. The study does not prove that meditators have universally better interoception or that a breathing sensor reads thoughts.

## Source 3 — Recent validity critique

**Citation:** Treves, I. N., et al. (2025). *Limited Validity of Breath-Counting as a Measure of Mindfulness*. Scientific Reports. DOI: [10.1038/s41598-025-22661-4](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-22661-4).

**Evidence class:** Recent peer-reviewed methodological critique.

**Use in this reel:** This source motivates caution about treating breath counting as a complete mindfulness measure. The reel does not state quantitative results from this paper beyond that its title and research question directly concern validity.

## Language and safety controls

Use “task-specific breath-counting accuracy,” “respiratory detection task,” “recorded respiratory signal,” “self-report,” and “in these samples.” Do not say “breath counting equals mindfulness,” “breathing sensors read thoughts,” “meditators always detect the body better,” or “slow breathing treats a condition.” No diagnosis, treatment, breathing prescription, individualized advice, guaranteed outcome, or universal claim will be given.
