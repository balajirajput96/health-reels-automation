# Reel 0035 — mood-congruent memory: studies वास्तव में क्या मापती हैं?

## Evidence question

Mood-congruent memory (MCM) refers to a possible bias in emotional memory toward material whose affective content matches a past or current mood. A study must specify where the mood appears in the memory process and what “memory” means: encoding, consolidation, retrieval, recognition accuracy, free recall, autobiographical recall, or false memory. These outcomes are not interchangeable.

## Measurement layers

| Layer | Example operationalization | Interpretation boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Mood state | Natural mood report or experimentally induced mood | A state measured at a particular time; not a permanent trait or diagnosis |
| Encoding | Memory for emotional material learned while in a mood | Tests acquisition under that condition |
| Consolidation/storage | Later memory after a delay | Requires an appropriate delay and design; often under-tested |
| Retrieval | Recall or recognition while in a mood | Can reflect search, accessibility, or response criteria |
| Autobiographical memory | Recall of personal events with affective tone | Depends on cueing, relevance, detail, and report accuracy |
| False memory | Endorsement or recall of mood-congruent lures | A congruent response is not automatically a true memory |
| Task reliability | Number and quality of category, association, recall, or recognition items | Weak or narrow tasks can make effects unstable or hard to generalize |

## Vetted scholarly sources

1. Faul, L., & LaBar, K. S. (2023). “Mood-congruent memory revisited.” *Psychological Review*, 130(6), 1421–1456. DOI: [10.1037/rev0000394](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000394); [PubMed PMID 36201828](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36201828/); [PMC10076454](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10076454/). This systematic review discusses mood and memory interactions in healthy participants and reports that MCM can appear as enhanced accuracy for previously encoded congruent content, preferential recall of congruent autobiographical events, or false memory for congruent lures. It also highlights conditions that shape the effect, possible mood-incongruent recall for mood repair, limited neuroimaging evidence, and the need for more consolidation research.

2. Matt, G. E., Vázquez, C., & Campbell, W. K. (1992). “Mood-congruent recall of affectively toned stimuli: A meta-analytic review.” *Clinical Psychology Review*, 12(2), 227–255. DOI: [10.1016/0272-7358(92)90116-P](https://doi.org/10.1016/0272-7358(92)90116-P); [ScienceDirect record](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/027273589290116P). The review examined mood-congruent recall across several mood and participant groups and considered self-referenced encoding and mood intensity. Its abstract reports different patterns across groups and caveats about interpretation, supporting a conditional rather than universal claim.

3. Mayer, J. D., McCormick, L. J., & Strong, S. E. (1995). “Mood-Congruent Memory and Natural Mood: New Evidence.” *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*, 21(7), 736–746. DOI: [10.1177/0146167295217008](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167295217008). The publisher abstract reports three studies of normal individuals in everyday moods with a total N=614 using category-retrieval and word-association tasks. The article also describes reliability concerns for a shorter item version, illustrating why the memory task itself matters.

## Claim boundaries

The reel will say that mood may bias what is accessed or reported under some conditions, not that mood decides what a person remembers. It will distinguish a mood induction from naturally occurring mood, encoding from retrieval, recognition from recall, true memory from false-memory endorsement, and a review’s synthesis from one person’s experience. It will avoid claims about depression or other diagnoses, will not infer the viewer’s memories, and will not prescribe a treatment or promise that changing mood will change memory.
