# Reel 0012 source record: mental models — what studies actually measure

## Evidence-bounded angle

A mental model is a theoretical representation of how someone understands relationships, mechanisms, or expected events. It is not directly visible as a picture in the head. Research therefore uses observable proxies: performance and reaction time on controlled reasoning problems, explanations or predictions, surveys of specific beliefs, interviews, card sorts, concept maps, and other elicitation methods. Different methods sample different parts of a representation and can require different inferences.

## Verified sources

### 1. Johnson-Laird (1980), foundational mental-model theory

- Citation: P. N. Johnson-Laird. “Mental Models in Cognitive Science.” *Cognitive Science*, 4(1), 71–115 (1980).
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog0401_4
- Publisher page: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1207/s15516709cog0401_4
- Evidence class: foundational theoretical article in cognitive science.
- What it supports: mental-model theory treats reasoning as involving internal representations of situations and relations. The theory motivates experiments that vary premises, relations, and conclusions, but the theory is not itself a direct measurement device.
- Boundary: this is a theory paper, not evidence that every mental representation is visual, complete, or measured by a single task.

### 2. Cortes et al. (2021), multidimensional relational-reasoning task

- Citation: Robert A. Cortes, Adam B. Weinberger, Griffin A. Colaizzi, Grace F. Porter, Emily L. Dyke, Holly O. Keaton, Dakota L. Walker & Adam E. Green. “What Makes Mental Modeling Difficult? Normative Data for the Multidimensional Relational Reasoning Task.” *Frontiers in Psychology*, 12, 668256 (2021).
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.668256
- Open full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8134533/
- Identifiers: PMCID: PMC8134533; PMID: 34025531.
- Evidence class: peer-reviewed behavioral measurement study.
- What it measured: participants judged whether conclusions followed from two- or three-premise problems. The Multidimensional Relational Reasoning Task systematically varied number of premises, number of relation dimensions, spatial versus non-spatial relations, truth/false/indeterminate solution type, premise order, and conclusion phrasing. The study analyzed accuracy and reaction time with mixed-effects models.
- What it supports: problem structure changes task difficulty. More premises and multidimensional relations can place greater demands on reasoning and working memory, and the task provides normative accuracy and reaction-time data.
- Boundary: accuracy and reaction time are behavioral indicators of performance under specific stimuli. They do not directly reveal a participant’s complete mental model or general reasoning ability in daily life.

### 3. Holtrop et al. (2021), mental-model elicitation and analysis methods

- Citation: Jodi Summers Holtrop, Laura D. Scherer, Daniel D. Matlock, Russell E. Glasgow & Lee A. Green. “The Importance of Mental Models in Implementation Science.” *Frontiers in Public Health*, 9, 680316 (2021).
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.3389/fpubh.2021.680316
- Open full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8290163/
- Identifiers: PMCID: PMC8290163; PMID: 34295871.
- Evidence class: peer-reviewed conceptual and methods review.
- What it supports: mental models can be defined as interrelated memories, conceptual knowledge, expectations, and causal beliefs. The review describes surveys, forced-choice tasks, card sorts, semi-structured interviews, cognitive task analysis, concept mapping, and related methods for eliciting or analyzing models. It emphasizes that some beliefs are tacit and that elicitation method affects what is captured.
- Boundary: the review’s examples come from implementation science and applied settings; methods can elicit reports, relationships, or expectations, but the resulting representation remains an interpretation shaped by the task and analysis.

## Measurement distinctions for the reel

A reasoning task can measure accuracy, errors, and reaction time under controlled changes in premises or relations. A survey can measure endorsement of specified beliefs, but is less suited to discovering complex relationships when the researcher does not already know the relevant belief range. An interview can reveal explanations and expectations, but answers are still reports shaped by language, memory, and the interview context. A card sort or concept map can reveal grouping, sequencing, or perceived relations. None of these alone is the whole mental model.

A safe conclusion is that mental-model research measures observable behavior or elicited representations and then makes a bounded inference about how people organize knowledge and expectations. It does not read a literal internal picture, prove that a model is fully accurate, or establish a universal “mental-model score.”
