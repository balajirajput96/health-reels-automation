# Reel 0052 retrieval audit: choice overload and what studies measure

## Retrieval scope

The retrieval focused on peer-reviewed work that separates assortment size from decision-task difficulty, choice-set complexity, preference uncertainty, decision goals, and the outcome used to define overload.

## Verified findings

**Chernev, Böckenholt, and Goodman (2015), Journal of Consumer Psychology, DOI 10.1016/j.jcps.2014.08.002.** This conceptual review and meta-analysis synthesized 99 observations from prior research involving 7,202 participants. It identified four moderators of the effect of assortment size: choice-set complexity, decision-task difficulty, preference uncertainty, and a prominent effort-minimizing goal. Higher levels of these moderators facilitated choice-overload effects. The paper also reports that satisfaction/confidence, regret, choice deferral, and switching likelihood were all used as outcome measures and were comparably useful indicators in the meta-analytic model. The conclusion is conditional: the effect of more options depends on how difficult and complex the decision is and what the chooser wants.

**Misuraca, Nixon, Miceli, Di Stefano, and Scaffidi Abbate (2024), Frontiers in Psychology, DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1290359.** This systematic qualitative review of 49 peer-reviewed empirical articles organizes moderators into decision-environment/context factors and decision-maker characteristics. It describes choice overload as possible negative consequences such as choice deferral, switching, regret, lower satisfaction, and lower confidence when the decision task exceeds available cognitive resources. It also summarizes evidence that choice can have benefits, including motivation and utility, and that option count alone does not determine the outcome. Attributes, time pressure, accountability, presentation format, option attractiveness, dominant options, and information chunking can alter difficulty and overload.

**Kaplan and Reed (2013), Behavioural Processes, DOI 10.1016/j.beproc.2013.04.001.** The study examined whether option discounting in choice overload could be explained only by temporal discounting and reward magnitude. The authors’ conclusion was that temporal discounting may contribute but is not the only process involved. This supports presenting mechanisms as hypotheses or partial explanations rather than as a complete account of why a large set changes choice behavior.

## Operational measure map

| Research observable | What it can describe | What it cannot establish alone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Number of options | Assortment size manipulation | That overload will occur |
| Choice deferral | Whether a choice was postponed | Why the choice was postponed |
| Switching likelihood | Whether people changed options | Lower decision quality in every context |
| Regret or satisfaction | Post-choice experience | Objective welfare or the best choice |
| Confidence | Subjective certainty | Accuracy or rationality |
| Decision time | Search or evaluation burden | Choice quality by itself |
| Preference uncertainty | How clear preferences are | A universal individual trait |
| Task complexity/time pressure | Structural decision difficulty | That option count was the causal driver alone |

## Evidence boundaries

“Choice overload” is not proof that more options are always harmful. Larger assortments can improve the chance of finding a preferred option, motivation, or utility, while overload is more likely under certain combinations of complexity, task difficulty, uncertain preferences, and goals. Consumer-assortment studies should not be generalized automatically to every life decision. The reel is public education only and does not make a personal financial, medical, or purchasing recommendation.
