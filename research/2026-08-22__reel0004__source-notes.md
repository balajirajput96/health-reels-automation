## Source 1 — peer-reviewed review

- **Title:** The Measurement of Individual Differences in Cognitive Biases: A Review and Improvement
- **Author:** Vincent Berthet
- **Journal/date:** *Frontiers in Psychology*, 2021; DOI `10.3389/fpsyg.2021.630177`
- **URL:** https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.630177/full
- **Evidence class:** peer-reviewed measurement review
- **Verified findings:** The review distinguishes objective measures based on performance on experimental tasks from self-report measures. It explains that some biases are measured against accuracy or a normative benchmark, while other effects are operationalized by comparing responses across conditions in which a normatively irrelevant factor is manipulated. It also reports that composite scores built from many single-item tasks can have low internal consistency, and that multi-item task designs can improve reliability. The review concludes that contextualized measures, in addition to generic measures, need improvement or development.
- **Limits:** This is a review of measurement practices, not evidence that any individual viewer has a bias or that a bias is a clinical assessment. Reported reliability values apply to the reviewed instruments and samples, not to all cognitive-bias tasks.

## Source 3 — peer-reviewed methodological study

- **Title:** Measuring Individual Differences in Decision Biases: Methodological Considerations
- **Authors/date:** Balazs Aczel, Barnabas Bago, Adam Szollosi, Anna Foldes, and Balazs Lukacs; *Frontiers in Psychology*, 19 November 2015, 6:1770; DOI `10.3389/fpsyg.2015.01770`; PMID `26635677`; PMCID `PMC4652008`
- **URL:** https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC4652008
- **Evidence class:** peer-reviewed methodological study
- **Verified findings:** Across two studies, the authors examined whether different versions of decision-bias tasks can be treated as interchangeable and combined into a common individual score. Study 1 modified 13 tasks and found weak internal consistency plus discrepancies in underlying factor patterns in 1,279 participants. Study 2 used three examples of each of seven biases in 527 participants and again questioned interchangeability. The paper emphasizes domain specificity and the effects of wording and response mode before combining tasks into multiple-bias questionnaires.
- **Limits:** The studies concern multi-bias questionnaire construction and individual-difference measurement; they do not show that every cognitive-bias experiment is invalid. Their findings should not be turned into a clinical assessment or a claim about a viewer.

## Source 4 — peer-reviewed psychometric study

- **Title:** Same same, but different: A psychometric examination of three frequently used experimental tasks for cognitive bias assessment in a sample of healthy young adults
- **Authors:** Alla Machulska, Kristian Kleinke, and Tim Klucken
- **Journal/date:** *Behavior Research Methods*, published 01 June 2022, volume 55, pages 1332–1351; DOI `10.3758/s13428-022-01804-9`
- **URL:** https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13428-022-01804-9
- **Evidence class:** peer-reviewed psychometric study
- **Verified findings:** The study repeatedly measured approach, attentional, and association biases in 79 healthy young adults using the Approach-Avoidance Task, visual dot-probe task, and Implicit Association Test at five time points. Reliability varied widely by task, device, and time point: the dot-probe task appeared unreliable, while some AAT and IAT versions showed moderate reliability. The study found almost no associations across information-processing tasks or between implicit and explicit measures, and recommends routinely reporting psychometric properties and matching the task to the precise research question.
- **Limits:** The sample was 79 healthy young adults, so results should not be generalized to all people or all tasks. This is a psychometric assessment, not a clinical assessment or a management study.

## Editorial boundary captured so far

A safe reel can explain that a study usually measures a response pattern in a defined task or contrast, not a person’s whole character, intelligence, or mental-health status. Any claim about reliability, context, or generalization must remain tied to the instrument, design, sample, and source limitations.
